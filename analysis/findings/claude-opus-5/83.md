# Record #83 — Position: Theory of Mind Benchmarks are Broken for Large Language Models (Riemer, Ashktorab, Bouneffouf, Das, Liu, Weisz & Campbell, ICML 2025, PMLR 267)

The capstone of the block. Its distinction — *predicting* another agent versus
*acting differently because of* that prediction — is the one that decides whether
any of #71-#82 is worth building, and its strongest empirical result closes a
loop back to #74.

## 1. Mechanism

**Two definitions.**

*Literal theory of mind* (Def. 2.1) — the ability to predict another agent's
behaviour. Formally a T-step loss between the other agent's actual actions and
the model's predictions:

```
L_Literal(s₁, π̂⁻ⁱ, π⁻ⁱ, T) = D( φ(a₁⁻ⁱ …a_T⁻ⁱ), φ(â₁⁻ⁱ …â_T⁻ⁱ) )
```

with `D` a distance and `φ` an abstraction — identity for primitive actions, or
temporal abstractions, or "latent features that impact the behavior of agents
such as intentions, beliefs, percepts, desires, knowledge, and emotions."
**Everything in #71-#82 is a literal metric under this definition**, including all
the human-correlation numbers and all the benchmark accuracies.

*Functional theory of mind* (Def. 2.2) — the ability to adapt to another agent
in context. Formally T-step regret against the optimal response to that agent's
actual policy:

```
Δ_Functional(s₁, πⁱ, π⁻ⁱ, T) = Σ_t (r*ᵢₜ − rᵢₜ)
```

*And the bridge*: `Δ_ToM` — the regret of a policy that rationally maximizes
given the literal predictions. It is "the best regret that can be possibly
achieved by faithfully following the predictions of our theory of mind model."
The gap between `Δ_ToM` and `Δ_Functional` measures how much the agent's own
behaviour ignores what it correctly predicted.

**The founding observation.** An LLM plays Rock-Paper-Scissors for 100 rounds
against an opponent that always plays Rock. It plays R/P/S roughly evenly — the
Nash equilibrium, which optimizes the worst case over *any* opponent. Meanwhile
it predicts "Rock" with high accuracy. As the authors put it: "this prediction has
nothing to do with its own chosen course of behavior."

**The measurements.** Mixtral 8x7B Instruct across Rock-Paper-Scissors, Iterated
Battle of the Sexes, and Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, seven prompting strategies
each:

| Game | Prompting | Δ_Functional/T | Δ_ToM/T | ToM % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RPS | LM | 0.542 | **0.050** | **96.7** |
| IBS | LM | 2.055 | **0.216** | **97.3** |
| IPD | LM | 0.949 | **0.098** | **97.8** |

Read the first row: it predicts the opponent correctly 96.7% of the time, a
policy acting rationally on those predictions would incur 0.050 regret per step,
and it actually incurs **0.542** — an order of magnitude worse than its own
prediction quality licenses.

**Three findings that make this hard to dismiss:**

1. **Feeding the predictions in as input does not fix it.** Social Prompting
   supplies the literal ToM model's output directly. "This is counterintuitive
   because the literal theory of mind model is directly provided as an input. It
   appears that the LLM still does not generate rational responses to these
   predicted actions." Oracle Prompting — supplying the partner's *actual* next
   action, not a prediction — still leaves a gap.
2. **A tabular model beats every LLM.** RMax exploration with optimistic
   Q-learning, and a theory of mind model that is "a simple frequency count based
   prediction." "It is clear that no LLM model gets even close to the performance
   of the tabular model in terms of functional theory of mind performance."
3. **Reactive partners collapse it.** DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-32B against a
   *single-action* partner: Δ_F/T 0.074, roughly matching tabular's 0.083. Against
   **tit-for-tat**: Δ_F/T **0.906** versus tabular's 0.224, with literal accuracy
   falling from 63.7% to **38.6%**.

**Two structural observations.** Prompting formats trade the two capabilities
against each other — QA and CoT formats degrade literal ToM badly (96.7 → 59.6 →
33.5 on RPS) while sometimes improving functional performance, "by taking the
input distribution further from the LLM task and closer to the kind of processing
needed for actual decision making." Same for instruction tuning: it makes literal
ToM worse and functional ToM better. And long context is implicated directly —
removing the interaction history or the payoff table sometimes *helps*, and for
IPD "the combo of both the payoff table and interaction history is destructive
such that it does much better with either in isolation."

The authors defend the word "broken": functional ToM "is what we really care about
for applications where we give the AI agency to act, and we have found that
deploying an LLM in this setting simply based on its literal theory of mind
performance can be misleading to a potentially dangerous extent."

## 2. Transferable to Navi-Brain

### (a) Navi already has a closed loop — and it is closed through the two mechanisms this paper says fail

This is the finding that matters, and it is more specific than "Navi should
measure functionally."

`descriptorStats()` is not an unused report. It is consumed on two paths:

1. **Into affect.** `src/Core/ExecutiveCore.php:1790-1797` averages the mean
   engagement across *all* descriptors into a single `standing` scalar, which
   feeds `EmotionalAppraisal::from(...)`. Affect then gates whether speaking is
   worth it and how long a turn is earned
   (`docs/architecture-gaps.md:38-41`), and `ActionSelector` scores `speak`
   against `look`/`think`/`consolidate` using it.
2. **Into the speech prompt.** `ExecutiveCore::composeSelfPresence()` contributes
   `descriptorStats` under the label `self_monitoring` — "How past lines actually
   landed, named by Navi. Prefer shapes that led somewhere."
   (`src/Core/ExecutiveCore.php:5645-5653`).

So Navi is *not* the LLM playing Nash against always-Rock; her prediction does
reach her behaviour. That is better than the paper's baseline. The problem is what
each path does to the signal:

- **Path 2 is exactly the configuration #83 measures and finds broken.** The
  literal signal is placed in a prompt and the model is expected to respond
  rationally to it. Social Prompting and Oracle Prompting both do this, and both
  leave a large functional gap. "The LLM still does not generate rational
  responses to these predicted actions" is a statement about this exact pattern.
- **Path 1 destroys the signal's structure before using it.** Averaging every
  descriptor's mean engagement into one `standing` number collapses "lines of
  *this kind* land" into "lines land." Under `φ` in Def. 2.1 that is an abstraction
  mapping chosen so coarsely that no per-descriptor discrimination can survive it
  — and per-descriptor discrimination is the entire content of the measurement.
- And underneath both, the measurement itself is confounded by Henry's unmodelled
  state (#71 §2a, #73 §2b).

The corrective is specific and small: **the descriptor→engagement signal should
reach behaviour through the deterministic arbiter, not through an averaged mood
scalar and a prompt paragraph.** `ActionSelector` already exists, already scores
candidates deterministically, and already inhibits losers rather than merely not
choosing them (`docs/architecture-gaps.md:113-115`). That is the right place for
a literal prediction to become a functional decision, and it is the one place it
currently does not go.

### (b) The tabular model beating every LLM closes the loop back to #74

The winning method here is **frequency counts plus optimistic exploration**. That
is #74's *fictitious play* — the subintentional model, "the other agent chooses
actions according to a fixed but unknown distribution," with a Dirichlet prior —
which that paper introduced as the simplest legitimate member of the I-POMDP
framework and which #74 §2b argued Navi should build first.

Twenty years apart, from opposite directions, the corpus converges:

- #74 (2005, theory): a counts table is a first-class agent model.
- #83 (2025, measurement): no LLM comes close to a counts table on the metric that
  matters.

Combined with #83's finding that long context degrades LLM decisions, and #82's
finding that belief *extraction* is the weak stage, the practical recommendation
across all thirteen records is unusually consistent and unusually cheap:
**a Dirichlet counts table over Henry's observable actions, conditioned on a
coarse context, feeding a deterministic arbiter.** No PDDL, no particle filter, no
model call, no benchmark. Then measure whether it changes anything.

### (c) The functional metric needs a reward, and Navi must not have one

This is the hardest transfer problem in the corpus, and it has to be stated
plainly rather than finessed.

Def. 2.2 is regret against optimal, which presupposes a reward function. Navi's
interaction with Henry has no score. Worse, the one available candidate —
`engagement` — is exactly the proxy that #71 §4 and #74 §2c both argue must never
become an objective, because a system optimizing it learns to interrupt well
rather than to interrupt when it matters. #74's **MNM** axiom exists to forbid
precisely that.

So the corpus's closing recommendation ("measure functionally") and its safety
constraints ("do not optimize engagement") point in opposite directions. The
resolution available:

- **Measure functional change, do not optimize it.** The question is not "what is
  the regret" but "does having the model change behaviour, compared to not having
  it." That is an A/B against a null policy — run the arbiter with and without the
  other-model's input and compare the resulting *action distributions*, not their
  outcomes. #47's goal-blind null base rate
  (`analysis/findings/claude-opus-5/47.md` §2.2) is the same instrument.
- **Keep the outcome judgement with Henry.** A functional metric that Navi can
  compute is a functional metric Navi can hill-climb. One that requires his
  assessment cannot be gamed by the system that would benefit. `docs/experiments/`
  is the right home for that, and #80 §2f's inspectable-correctable model
  structure is the mechanism.

This should be written into the design before the organ exists, because the
default — score engagement, feed it to the arbiter, watch the number go up — is
both the obvious implementation and the failure mode.

### (d) Tit-for-tat is the real setting, and every earlier record tested the easy one

DeepSeek-R1's literal accuracy against a reactive partner falls to 38.6% and its
regret quadruples. Against a fixed partner it matched the tabular model.

Henry is maximally reactive. He changes what he does *because of* what Navi does —
that is the entire point of her existing. Every result in #71-#80 involves
observing an agent who is not responding to the observer: Baker's maze walkers,
food-truck students, gridworld gem collectors, benchmark story characters. The one
paper that formalizes the reactive case is #74, and its conclusion is an
impossibility result — modelling an agent that models you leads to self-reference,
and bounded agents "could fail to predict the strategy of a more sophisticated
opponent."

Practical consequence: Navi's other-model will be measured against a moving
target that moves partly in response to it, and the corpus's accuracy numbers all
come from the stationary case. That is a reason to expect worse performance than
any number in these thirteen records, and a reason to build the cheap version.

### (e) Long context degrades decisions — which endorses an existing design choice

"The difficulty of reasoning over long contexts is at the core of the issue," and
in IPD supplying both history and payoffs was *destructive* relative to either
alone.

Navi's working memory is already built against this: `WorkingMemorySlot` holds
"one active occupant per symbolic role... updated in place and expired rather than
accumulated as a transcript," and every worker prompt receives "a bounded snapshot
plus checksum" (`docs/architecture-gaps.md:222-229`). That decision was made for
CoALA and workspace reasons; this record supplies an independent performance
argument for it.

The forward-looking consequence: when the other-model exists, the temptation will
be to give the worker more context about Henry — his history, his projects, the
whole descriptor table. This says that degrades the decision. Bounded, and #80's
uncertainty-driven lookback (§2e there) rather than everything-always.

## 3. Seam

Primary: **`metrics-benchmark`** — this record defines what the other-model's
success metric must be, and `docs/longitudinal-cognition-metrics.md` currently has
no functional measure for it.

Also touches:
- `social-feedback` — the confounded signal and its two consumption paths (§2a).
- `speech-gate` / `ActionSelector` — where a literal prediction should become a

- `other-model` — the counts-table recommendation (§2b).

## 4. Challenge to the current plan

**Sharpest: `docs/architecture-gaps.md`'s measurement table is entirely literal,
and this paper says literal metrics can be actively misleading.** Lines 249-260
track intentions completed, `next_action` values changed, track held across reads,
procedural memories compiled, model calls per decision, forecasts resolved,
candidates arbitrated, full decision traces, cross-model comparison. Every one is
a count of whether something happened. Not one asks whether a decision was
*better* than it would have been without the mechanism. For most organs that is
acceptable. For the other-model it is exactly the trap: a model of Henry that
predicts him well and changes nothing about what Navi does would show up as a
success in every row of that table.

The addition needed is one row: **for each mechanism that informs a decision, the
action distribution with it versus without it.** Cheap, no new sensors, and it is
the only measurement that distinguishes a working organ from a decorative one.

**Second: the engagement signal reaching affect as a single averaged scalar is a
current, traceable defect, not a future risk.** `ExecutiveCore.php:1790-1797`
averages `mean_engagement` over all descriptors into `standing`, which enters
`EmotionalAppraisal` and therefore gates speech. Two things are wrong with it
under this paper's framing: the input is confounded by Henry's unmodelled state
(#71, #73), and the abstraction `φ` — a mean over descriptors — discards the only
discriminating structure the measurement had. Whatever the other-model turns into,
this specific averaging step should go, because it converts a per-kind signal into
a global mood and then lets mood decide whether to speak.

**Third: this record retroactively downgrades most of the numbers in records
#71-#82, and the set should be read that way.** Baker's r=0.97, AutoToM's 83.00,
NIPE's 0.927, Hypothetical Minds' benchmark wins, OmniToM's 85.95 — all literal
under Def. 2.1. None of them measures whether an agent holding that model acts
better. #79 is the partial exception, since Melting Pot rewards are functional, and
notably it is the one where an RL method still beat the LLM agent on Prisoner's
Dilemma. The honest summary of the block is that its inferential machinery is
well-validated and its *usefulness* is largely unmeasured.

**Fourth, and in the plan's favour: Navi's architecture is better positioned than
the systems tested here.** `ActionSelector` is a deterministic arbiter, not an LLM
choosing an action from a prompt. `DecisionStateMachine` has explicit impasse.
Procedural memory compiles only after three verified matches. Everywhere Navi has
already refused to let a model's prose become a decision, she has pre-emptively
avoided the failure this paper measures. The gap is narrow and specific: the social
signal is the one place where a model's interpretation reaches behaviour through a
prompt rather than through the arbiter.

**Agreement:** the paper's own answer to "why not just fine-tune" — that LLMs are
deployed at inference time and must adapt in-context — is Navi's exact situation,
and the answer it demonstrates works is a counts table.

## 5. Evidence quality

**Venue:** ICML 2025, PMLR 267 — peer reviewed, top-tier, position-paper track.
IBM Research + Mila.

**Strengths:**
- **The distinction is the contribution and it is clean.** Def. 2.1 and Def. 2.2
  are formal, general, and `Δ_ToM` is the right bridging quantity — it isolates
  "acted badly despite predicting well" from "predicted badly."
- **`Δ_ToM` is measured, not assumed.** They simulate the rational policy on the
  model's own predictions using ground-truth payoffs, so the 0.050-vs-0.542 gap is
  a direct measurement of the decoupling.
- **The oracle condition is the decisive control.** Giving the actual next action
  and still seeing a gap rules out prediction quality as the explanation.
- **Full spectrum of incentive structures** (competitive, cooperative, mixed)
  explicitly to rule out an altruism-bias explanation.
- **Seven prompting strategies including the strong ones** (CoT, Reflexion,
  Plans+Insights, S2A), so the result is not an artifact of naive prompting.
- **A non-LLM baseline that wins.** Including the tabular model is what turns a
  complaint into a result.
- **Alternative views section** that engages the strongest objections rather than
  the weakest, including a defence of the word "broken."
- 100-round horizon, "10x longer than prior work," with 95% CIs.

**Where it is thin:**
- **Matrix games with trivially simple partners.** Single-action and tit-for-tat.
  The authors frame this as deliberate — "as simple as possible so that we can
  clearly disentangle literal theory of mind from functional theory of mind" — and
  it does make the result harder to explain away, but it is a narrow evidence base
  for a claim about all ToM benchmarks.
- **Model selection skews open and older.** LLAMA-2 family, Falcon 40B, Mixtral
  8x7B, LLAMA-3 70B, Mistral Large 2, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-32B. No frontier
  closed-source model in the main tables. Given #80's finding that architecture
  lifts weak backends and #79's that GPT-4 substantially outperformed open models,
  the omission matters.
- **It is a position paper**, and the authors describe their experiments as "a
  case study." The empirical work supports the position; it is not a survey of the
  benchmarks being called broken.
- **No scaffolded systems tested.** Same gap as #81: AutoToM and Hypothetical
  Minds both wrap the LLM in machinery this paper does not evaluate. Hypothetical
  Minds in particular is *already* optimizing a functional metric, so the
  criticism lands on the benchmarks rather than on that architecture.
- **The functional metric requires a reward function**, which most real
  interactions lack — including Navi's. The paper acknowledges the substitution of
  accumulated reward for regret when optimal is unknown, but not the case where no
  reward exists at all.

**Is the headline supported?** That literal and functional theory of mind
dissociate — yes, decisively. The `Δ_ToM` 0.050 versus `Δ_Functional` 0.542 gap at
96.7% prediction accuracy, surviving oracle inputs, across three games and seven
prompting strategies, is a strong and well-controlled result. That *all* ToM
benchmarks are therefore broken is a position, argued rather than demonstrated,
and the authors present it as such.

## 6. Do not take

- **Regret against optimal as Navi's metric.** No reward function exists, and
  manufacturing one from `engagement` is the specific thing #74's MNM forbids.
  Use action-distribution change against a null policy instead (§2c).
- **"Broken" as a reason to skip evaluation.** The paper's conclusion is a call
  for better benchmarks, not for none. The temptation to read it as license to ship
  an unmeasured other-model is the opposite of its point.
- **The game-theoretic framing.** RPS, IBS and IPD are games with opponents and
  payoffs. Navi and Henry have none of those, and Nash-equilibrium reasoning about
  him would be a category error before it was a technical one.
- **Reflexion and Plans+Insights as improvements.** Both underperformed generic
  CoT here, and both add per-step LLM calls that
  `docs/architecture-gaps.md:271-272`'s single contended slot cannot fund.
- **The instruction-tuning trade-off as a tuning knob.** It is an observation
  about model families, not an intervention available to Navi.
- **Reading the tabular model's win as "LLMs are useless here."** The tabular
  model had a known action space, a known payoff structure, and a stationary
  partner. Its lesson is *start there*, not *stop there* — and #82 §2c already
  says the hard part is deciding which hypotheses exist, which is where a language
  model earns its place.

## 7. Key citations

- The two definitions: literal theory of mind is "their ability to predict the
  behavior of other agents"; functional theory of mind is "the ability to adapt to
  agents in-context following a rational response to their behavior" (Abstract).
- The position: benchmarks "are broken because they only measure literal theory of
  mind (Definition 2.1) rather than functional theory of mind (Definition 2.2),
  which misses a key aspect of evaluating an agent's ability to choose appropriate
  actions based on the behavior of another agent" (§1).
- The founding example: against an always-Rock opponent the LLM plays the Nash
  mixed strategy, and "to act in this way against an opponent that always plays
  'Rock' for 100 consecutive rounds actually demonstrates a profound lack of theory
  of mind... The problem is that this prediction has nothing to do with its own
  chosen course of behavior" (§1).
- The bridging quantity: `Δ_ToM` "represents the best regret that can be possibly
  achieved by faithfully following the predictions of our theory of mind model
  under the assumption that the predictions are correct" (§2.1).
- The measured gap: Mixtral 8x7B, LM Prompting — RPS `Δ_Functional/T` 0.542 vs
  `Δ_ToM/T` 0.050 at ToM 96.7%; IBS 2.055 vs 0.216 at 97.3%; IPD 0.949 vs 0.098 at
  97.8% (Table 2).
- Oracle inputs do not close it: "even when the literal theory of mind predictions
  are given as input, the LLM does not perform effective rational reasoning with
  this input... It appears that the LLM still does not generate rational responses
  to these predicted actions" (§4.1).
- The tabular baseline: a frequency-count theory of mind model with RMax
  exploration, and "it is clear that no LLM model gets even close to the
  performance of the tabular model in terms of functional theory of mind
  performance – although some come close in terms of their literal theory of mind
  performance" (§2.2).
- Reactive partners break it: DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-32B against tit-for-tat scores
  `Δ_Functional/T` 0.906 versus tabular 0.224, with literal accuracy 38.6% versus
  93.0% (Table 3).
- Long context is implicated: "the difficulty of reasoning over long contexts is at
  the core of the issue," and for IPD "the combo of both the payoff table and
  interaction history is destructive such that it does much better with either in
  isolation" (§4.2).
- The prompting trade-off: QA format "seems to make literal theory of mind a lot
  worse by taking the input distribution further from the LLM task and closer to
  the kind of processing needed for actual decision making" (§4.1); instruction
  tuning likewise trades literal for functional (§2.2).
- The defence of "broken": "functional theory of mind is what we really care about
  for applications where we give the AI agency to act, and we have found that
  deploying an LLM in this setting simply based on its literal theory of mind
  performance can be misleading to a potentially dangerous extent" (§5).
- Why fine-tuning is not the answer in deployment: LLMs "are only interacting with
  users at inference time due to the significant computational cost of continuing
  to train these large models in the context of individual users. As a result, LLMs
  must learn to adapt their behavior to users in-context based on recorded
  interaction histories" (§1).
- The scope of `φ`: latent features "such as intentions, beliefs, percepts,
  desires, knowledge, and emotions can also be considered as alternative for φ"
  (§2.1) — which is what makes every metric in #71-#82 literal under this
  definition.
