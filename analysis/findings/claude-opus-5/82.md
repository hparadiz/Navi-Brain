# Record #82 — OmniToM: Benchmarking Theory of Mind in LLMs via Explicit Belief Modeling (Bawatneh, Sapkota, Bedi, Karmaker & Shah, arXiv:2605.26322)

Second guardrail. Where #81 attacked benchmark validity from cognitive theory,
this attacks it from measurement: stop scoring the final answer, score the belief
structure. Its schema is directly reusable as a storage format for Navi, and its
two worst-performing schema dimensions are exactly the two her safety invariants
require.

## 1. Mechanism

**The complaint.** Endpoint QA — story in, multiple-choice answer out — "leaves
the underlying mental-state representation unobserved. A model may answer a
false-belief question correctly, while failing to represent the actor-specific
mental states that make the answer valid."

**The fix: two graded stages.**

*Stage 1 — Belief Extraction.* `f_ext(S) → B` where
`B = {(a_i, b_i, o_i)}`:
- `a_i` — the actor holding the belief, **or the special actor `world`**, which
  marks narrated facts "not attributed to any single character's internal state."
- `b_i` — "the minimal content being represented."
- `o_i ∈ {0,1,2,3}` — recursive order, with **0 reserved for world-level facts**.

Extraction is explicitly recursive: world facts first, then actors, then each
actor's first-order beliefs, then higher-order beliefs about others' beliefs.

*Stage 2 — Belief Labeling.* Each proposition gets a seven-dimensional label
vector, grounded in ATOMS (Abilities in Theory of Mind Space):

| Dimension | What it records |
|---|---|
| Order | recursive depth |
| Truth Status | relation to reality |
| **Knowledge Access** | **who can access or share the information** |
| **Representation** | **stated versus inferred** |
| Content Type | subject matter |
| Mental Source | how it was acquired |
| Context | special framing conditions |

**Scale.** 895 stories derived from ToMBench, 22,343 labeled belief propositions,
"over 1K person-hours of human annotation effort" before a human-calibrated
LLM-assisted pipeline was fixed and scaled. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 was selected as the
generator (72.88 Stage 1 F1, 93.62 Stage 2 accuracy in calibration); GPT-5 serves
as the semantic judge for Stage 1 and is therefore excluded from Stage 1 scoring.

**Results** (zero-shot, nine models from 8B to 123B plus closed-source):

- **Stage 1 extraction peaks at 57.69 macro F1.**
- **Stage 2 labeling peaks at 85.95%** (Gemini-2.5 Flash; GPT-5 82.85%).

The 28-point gap between the two is the finding. Given a belief proposition,
models classify it competently. Given a story, they extract the wrong set of
beliefs.

**Where Stage 2 fails is more specific and more interesting.** Errors concentrate
on **Knowledge Access (56.13-74.59%)** and **Representation (54.33%+)** — "the
dimensions that specify who could know or share a belief and whether it is stated
or inferred." Every other dimension sits in the 80s or 90s (Order 95.56, Truth
Status 84.97, Context 92.14).

**And the bottleneck is not recursion depth.** Order-1 actor beliefs have the
*lowest* overall labeling accuracy (71.2%), with Knowledge Access at 58.9% and
Representation at 57.3%. The hard step is **turning a world fact into somebody's
belief** — the first step — not nesting beliefs inside beliefs.

The authors name it: "current LLMs can often recover story facts, but struggle to
determine how those facts are distributed across actors, communicated or
inferred, and transformed into beliefs."

## 2. Transferable to Navi-Brain

### (a) The two dimensions models fail are the two Navi must have — so derive them, don't generate them

This is the sharpest practical finding in the record. Knowledge Access and
Representation are the two schema fields LLMs handle worst (55-75% versus 85-95%
everywhere else). They are also the two that Navi's existing invariants make
mandatory:

- **Knowledge Access** — who could know this — is #72's isovist. Did Henry hear
  the line? Was he at the machine? Was he in a meeting?
- **Representation** — stated versus inferred — is #74's **MNO** axiom. Models of
  other agents are never observed, only inferred, and an attribution must never be
  storable as an observation.

The conclusion follows directly: **compute these two fields from the sensor
record; never let a model label them.** Navi is in an unusually good position to
do so, because unlike a model reading a story, she *was there*:

- Knowledge Access ← `DesktopAwareness::presence()` (`using_computer`,
  `active_within_seconds`), `HearingStream` meeting detection, whether the Pet
  actuator actually played the line, `UtteranceOutcome.present_at_utterance`.
- Representation ← provenance. If it came from `HearingStream` as his own words,
  it is `stated`. Anything else is `inferred`, by construction, with a link to the
  observation that produced it.

Both are deterministic lookups over data already stored. The benchmark says the
generative path is close to a coin flip on exactly these; the sensor path is
exact. That is an architecture decision this record makes for free.

### (b) The `world` actor is a one-column schema change with a large safety payoff

Separating order-0 world facts from actor beliefs by an explicit `actor` field —
with `world` as a reserved value — is the cleanest expression of a distinction
Navi's memory currently cannot make.

Right now `Memory` in the semantic tier holds claims about the world with no
actor. "The heartbeat is stalled" and "Henry thinks the heartbeat is stalled" have
the same shape and the same storage. Once an other-model exists, that is a live
hazard: `memory:consolidate` promotes episode-derived takeaways into the semantic
tier, and `docs/architecture-gaps.md:31-33` records what happened the one time
unsourced material went through that path — "confident inventions about mechanisms
that do not exist."

Adding `actor` (default `world`) plus `order` to memory records makes it
*structurally impossible* to consolidate an attribution about Henry into a fact
about the machine. That is worth doing before the other-model exists rather than
after, and it is a migration, not a subsystem.

### (c) The extraction/labeling gap measures the problem the whole block deferred

57.69 versus 85.95 is the quantitative version of what #75, #76 and #78 all set
aside — "leaving aside how people come up with these contextual hypotheses."
Deciding *which* mental states are relevant is much harder than reasoning about
them once chosen, and this is the first record in the block to put a number on it.

For Navi that reorders the risk. The failure mode to expect is not mislabeling
what Henry believes; it is generating a belief set that misses what matters. Two
consequences:

- #79's hypothesis generation is the fragile step, not its Rescorla-Wagner
  scoring. Instrument the generator, not just the scores.
- #80's `R = −H` stopping rule is worse than it looks here: a model that extracted
  the wrong belief structure will still produce a confident answer over it.
  Together with #81 §2a, this is the third independent argument that confidence
  cannot be the gate.

### (d) Order 1 is the bottleneck, which cuts both ways for the depth-1 plan

#74 §2d and #73 §6 both recommended a depth-1 other-model: Navi models Henry, not
Henry's model of Navi. This record says recursion depth is *not* where the
difficulty lives — Order-1 beliefs score worse than higher orders on the
dimensions that matter.

Good news: skipping depth 2 skips little. Bad news: **the one step Navi needs is
the hard one.** "Henry believes X" is precisely the transformation with 58.9%
Knowledge Access accuracy. That is a direct argument for §2a — the first-order
step is where the derived fields have to carry the weight, because the generative
path is weakest exactly there.

### (e) A minimal proposition is a better memory unit than a sentence

"The minimal content being represented" is a discipline worth importing. Navi's
`UtteranceOutcome.response_text` stores up to 600 characters of concatenated
reply text as an opaque blob. A belief-proposition format — one actor, one minimal
claim, one order, plus derived access and provenance labels — turns that blob into
something queryable by the ranked lexical search that already exists, and
something that can be individually scored, expired, or contradicted.

## 3. Seam

Primary: **`memory-tier`** — this record is mostly a schema proposal.

Also touches:
- `other-model` — the belief-proposition format is its storage unit.
- `self-model` — `Representation` (stated vs inferred) is MNO made enforceable.
- `perception` — Knowledge Access derived from presence, hearing, and actuator
  state (§2a).
- `metrics-benchmark` — extraction versus labeling as separately scored stages.
- `worker-schema` — what a parser worker is allowed to emit, and which fields it
  is forbidden from filling.

## 4. Challenge to the current plan

**Sharpest: Navi's memory schema has no actor field, and that becomes a
correctness bug the moment the other-model ships.** The consolidation path is
designed and working; `memory:consolidate` promotes episode takeaways into the
semantic tier with inherited lineage. Nothing in that path can distinguish a claim
about the world from a claim about a person's mind, because the schema does not
carry the distinction. Every mitigation currently protecting semantic memory is
about *sourcing* (evidence required, Navi's own speech excluded) and none is about
*attribution*. This is a small migration that has to precede the organ, not follow
it.

**Second: the plan's instinct will be to have the model label everything, and the
data says that is worst exactly where it matters.** A natural implementation asks
a bounded worker to emit a structured belief record with all fields populated —
that is what `FreeModelWorker`'s schema-valid-JSON contract encourages. This
record says two of those fields would be filled at 55-75% accuracy by a model,
and both are the safety-relevant ones. The worker contract for the other-model
should therefore *forbid* certain fields rather than merely validate them: the
parser emits actor and content; the executive fills access and provenance from the
sensor record. That is a stronger contract than "schema-valid," and it is new.

**Third: the benchmark's own construction limits what its numbers can bear.**
Gold labels were generated by Claude-Sonnet-4.5 after human calibration, and
Stage 1 is scored by a GPT-5 semantic judge whose **human-alignment agreement is
72.03%** — which the authors flag: "Stage 1 F1 should be treated as an approximate
aggregate measure, especially for close model comparisons." So the headline 57.69
is measured by an instrument that disagrees with humans more than a quarter of the
time, against gold labels an LLM produced. The *structural* findings — extraction
lags labeling, Knowledge Access and Representation are the weak dimensions —
survive that noise because the gaps are large and consistent across nine models.
The specific numbers do not.

**Fourth: the scope excludes Navi's actual situation, explicitly.** The
limitations name "long-horizon information tracking" and "dense temporal
structure" as out of scope, and the stories are "short, self-contained
narratives." Navi's problem is continuous, multi-day, and has no narrative
boundary. This benchmark measures a strictly easier version of the task, which
makes 57.69 a ceiling rather than an estimate.

**Agreement:** the two-stage split — extract structure, then label it — matches
how Navi already separates observables from interpretation. `UtteranceOutcome`'s
schema comment does exactly this, splitting observable columns from the
free-form `descriptor` the reflective pass chooses. The instinct is already in the
codebase; this record generalizes it.

## 5. Evidence quality

**Venue:** arXiv preprint (2605.26322v1, 25 May 2026), marked "Preprint," 30
pages. Not peer reviewed.

**Strengths:**
- **Scale and effort**: 895 stories, 22,343 propositions, over 1,000 person-hours
  of human annotation before the pipeline was fixed.
- **Nine models across a wide size range** (8B to 123B, plus closed-source), so
  the structural findings are not one model's quirk.
- **The per-dimension breakdown is the value.** An aggregate accuracy would have
  read as "85.95%, pretty good"; the breakdown localizes the failure to two
  specific dimensions and to Order-1 beliefs.
- **Annotation hygiene**: closed label space, three-annotator verification,
  majority voting, expert adjudication.
- **The limitations section is unusually honest**, including publishing the
  judge's 72.03% agreement rather than burying it.
- The judge model does not win the benchmark (GPT-5 82.85 vs Gemini-2.5 Flash
  85.95), which is mild evidence against judge bias in Stage 2.

**Where it is thin:**
- **LLM-generated gold labels graded by an LLM judge.** Claude-Sonnet-4.5 produced
  the benchmark; GPT-5 scores Stage 1 at 72.03% human agreement. Human calibration
  covers a subset. This is disclosed and it still means the ground truth is
  partly model output.
- **Stage 1 F1 is explicitly "approximate"** by the authors' own instruction, which
  undercuts the headline 57.69.
- **Text-only, short, self-contained stories.** No multimodality, no interaction,
  no long-horizon tracking, no nesting past order 3.
- **Inherits ToMBench's topical and representational limits**, acknowledged.
- **Schema subjectivity** on seven interpretive dimensions, mitigated but not
  eliminated.
- **Zero-shot only.** No scaffolded or model-based systems tested, so this says
  nothing about whether #80's architecture would fix the extraction gap — which is
  the obvious next experiment and the one most relevant to Navi.
- **No human baseline on the benchmark itself.** We are told models reach 57.69 F1
  on extraction; we are not told what people score, so "bottleneck" is relative to
  an unmeasured ceiling.

**Is the headline supported?** The structural claim — endpoint QA hides
representational failure, and the failure localizes to distributing facts across
actors — is well supported by a consistent pattern across nine models and two
independently-scored stages. The precise numbers rest on a moderately-aligned LLM
judge over partly LLM-generated gold labels.

## 6. Do not take

- **57.69 or 85.95 as capability estimates.** Approximate by the authors'
  instruction, on short stories, with no human baseline.
- **The full seven-dimensional schema.** Navi needs four fields at most — actor,
  proposition, knowledge-access, stated-vs-inferred — plus provenance. Content
  Type, Mental Source and Context are analysis dimensions for a benchmark, and
  every extra field is another thing a model would be asked to guess.
- **LLM labeling of Knowledge Access or Representation.** §2a: derive both. This
  is the record's main operational consequence.
- **Belief order beyond 1.** Confirmed unnecessary here by a different route than
  #74 — the difficulty is at order 1, so depth costs without buying.
- **An LLM-generated gold standard for Navi's own evaluation.** If a probe set for
  the other-model gets built (#81 §2e), its ground truth should come from Henry or
  from unambiguous machine state, not from a model labelling a transcript.
- **Reading Stage 2's 85.95% as "models understand beliefs."** They classify
  supplied propositions well. The supplying is the hard part, and it scores 28
  points lower.

## 7. Key citations

- The complaint about endpoint QA: it "obscures whether the model actually
  constructs the underlying mental-state representations required for robust
  reasoning" and "leaves the underlying mental-state representation unobserved. A
  model may answer a false-belief question correctly, while failing to represent
  the actor-specific mental states that make the answer valid" (Abstract, §1).
- The extraction format: `B = {(a_i, b_i, o_i)}`, where `a_i` is "either the actor
  (character or group) holding the belief or the special actor `world`, which marks
  narrated facts not attributed to any single character's internal state,"
  `b_i` is "the minimal content being represented," and `o_i ∈ {0,1,2,3}` with "0
  reserved for world-level facts" (§3).
- The seven dimensions: "recursive order, truth status, knowledge access,
  explicitness, content type, mental source, and context" (Abstract), grounded in
  ATOMS.
- The two headline numbers: "Stage 2 belief-labeling accuracy reaches 85.95%,
  while Stage 1 extraction F1 peaks at 57.69%" (§1).
- Where the errors live: models "are weakest on Knowledge Access (56.13-74.59%)
  and Representation (54.33-...)," the dimensions "that specify who could know or
  share a belief and whether it is stated or inferred" (§ Main Results).
- The bottleneck is at order 1, not deeper: "Order 1 actor beliefs have the lowest
  overall labeling accuracy (71.2%), with especially low accuracy for Knowledge
  Access (58.9%) and Representation (57.3%)."
- The diagnosis: "current LLMs can often recover story facts, but struggle to
  determine how those facts are distributed across actors, communicated or
  inferred, and transformed into beliefs" (Conclusion).
- The judge caveat, in the authors' words: "Stage 1 scoring uses a
  human-calibrated semantic judge rather than exhaustive human adjudication; its
  72.03% human-alignment agreement is moderate, so Stage 1 F1 should be treated as
  an approximate aggregate measure, especially for close model comparisons"
  (Limitations).
- Scope exclusions: the benchmark does "not cover multimodal or interactive
  reasoning, long-horizon information tracking, dense temporal structure, or belief
  nesting beyond order 3" (Limitations).
- Construction: "895 stories derived from ToMBench... augmented with 22,343
  labeled belief propositions," supported by "over 1K person-hours of human
  annotation effort," with Claude-Sonnet-4.5 selected as the generator (72.88
  Stage 1 F1, 93.62 Stage 2 accuracy in calibration).
