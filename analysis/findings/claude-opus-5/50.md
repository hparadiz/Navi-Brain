# Record #50 — Sigaud et al., A Definition of Open-Ended Learning for Goal-Conditioned Agents

## 1. Mechanism

This paper contains no algorithm, no system, and no experiment. It is a
definitional/position paper whose entire product is a lattice of fourteen
numbered definitions (Figure 2, p. 7) split into *problem* definitions (blue)
and *solution/agent* definitions (red). Mechanically, what it offers is a
predicate you can evaluate over a log, plus two named degenerate cases.

**The base construction (p. 8).**

- `Definition Pro1`: "A process is an entity that generates a set of tokens
  through time." No constraint on what a token is, no time limit, repeats
  allowed, several tokens may be emitted at once (p. 8).
- `Key definition 1`: "An observer considers a process as open-ended if, for any
  time t, there exists a time t' > t at which the process generates a token that
  is new according to this observer's perspective." (p. 8)

Two things carry all the weight here. First, novelty is **observer-relative and
explicitly so**: the observer "might be a non-transparent subjective evaluator
(e.g., a human user) or alternatively an objective mechanism that incorporates
some measure of novelty (e.g., an algorithm)" (p. 8). The predicate is
undefined until you name the observer. Second, they prove the consequence: if
`|Tok_t|` is the count of distinct tokens by time t, then for any N there is a
t' with `|Tok_t'| >= |Tok_t| + N`, so an open-ended process generates an
unbounded number of distinct tokens (p. 8). Open-endedness therefore entails
unbounded distinct-token storage if you want to check novelty exactly.

**Lifting to RL and GCRL (p. 9).**

- `Pro2`: an RL problem is `<O, A, T, R>` with `T : H_t -> Delta(O)` and
  `R : H_t -> IR`, over histories `H = union_t (O x A)^t` (p. 9). They use Abel
  et al.'s history formalism rather than MDPs because it is broader.
- `Key definition 2`: an open-ended RL problem is a *sequence* of RL problems in
  which any one of the following is generated by an open-ended process: **the
  observation space, the action space or some hierarchical options, the
  transition function, the reward function, or the RL problems themselves**
  (p. 9). This is a five-slot checklist of "what is allowed to grow", and it is
  the most directly auditable artifact in the paper.
- `Pro3`: a GCRL problem is `<O, A, T, G, R>` with `R : H_t x G -> IR`; goals
  are elements of an arbitrary space `G` and the paper deliberately does **not**
  say where they come from (p. 9).
- `Key definition 3`: "A GCRL problem is an open-ended GCRL problem if the goals
  come from an open-ended generation process." (p. 9)

They immediately flag the finite-lifespan escape hatch: a generator with a
finite distinct-goal set cannot be open-ended, "however, in practice agents
generally have a finite lifespan. Thus a process generating a finite set of
goals might be seen as giving rise to an open-ended goal generation process even
if the infinite horizon condition is not met" (p. 9).

**The degenerate-case diagnostic (p. 10) — the only genuinely operational part.**

They name two ways to satisfy Key definition 3 while learning nothing:

- **Case 1, discrete but infinite goal space.** "the goal is to count from 1 to
  N... Generating as goals an infinite sequence of growing values of N is enough
  for that problem to be open-ended" (p. 10).
- **Case 2, continuous goal space.** "asking a physical agent to travel at a
  given speed... Targeting higher and higher traveling speeds that converge
  asymptotically to the highest possible traveling speed of the agent is a
  problem that already verifies the open-endedness property" (p. 10).

These "qualify as open-ended GCRL problems for naive observers who do not
require a strong notion of novelty" (p. 10). Two proposed remedies, stated but
not formalised:

1. Raise the observer's bar — "Demanding observers may require new tokens to be
   'substantially new' or 'interestingly new'" (p. 10). No measure is given.
2. Add an order distinction:
   - `Pro4` **first-order** open-ended GCRL: all goals `g_i` sampled from the
     same goal space `G` (p. 10).
   - `Pro5` **second-order**: "a first open-ended process generates an open-ended
     sequence of goal representation spaces `Omega_j ⊂ Omega`, and a second
     open-ended process generates goals `g_i,j` within each generated space"
     (p. 10).

   They then concede the fix is incomplete: "second-order open-ended GCRL
   problems can still generate a trivial diversity of goal spaces, so again we
   have to add further conditions" (p. 10). No such conditions are supplied.

**Agent-side definitions (pp. 11-13).** `Sol1` RL agent `lambda : H_t ->
Delta(A)`; `Sol2` GCRL agent `kappa : H_t x G -> Delta(A)`; `Sol3` an open-ended
RL agent is one that solves an open-ended RL problem (p. 11, near-vacuous);
`Sol4` an open-ended GCRL agent uses goals from an open-ended goal generation
process (p. 11); `Sol5` **lifelong** = open-ended and "does not forget how to
solve RL problems or achieve goals when it learns how to solve other RL problems"
(p. 12). Then a three-way taxonomy of goal origin: `Sol6` **extrinsic** (goal
generation external to the agent, the gym `GoalEnv` case), `Sol7` **autotelic**
(goal generation "fully internal to the agent"), both p. 12; `Sol8`
**teachable** — "its internal goal generation process can be influenced by other
agents" via "demonstrations, descriptions, instructions or feedback" (p. 13).
They note the curriculum heuristic from the autotelic literature: expand
behavioural goals by sampling from already-achieved goals, and "the most
efficient curricula consist in sampling at the frontier of the domain of
currently achieved goals" (p. 12).

**The decision procedure they actually offer (pp. 13-14).** Section 5 asks "How
can we make sure that a learning agent is an OEL agent?" and gives two tests:

- **Two-phase protocol (Cartoni and Baldassarre 2018), p. 13.** Split the
  agent's life into a long *intrinsic phase* with "no learning guidance (e.g.,
  reward functions or goals) nor any hardwired knowledge", then an *extrinsic
  phase* tested on tasks "randomly drawn" from the same environment and
  representative of all tasks it could generate. Extrinsic-phase performance is
  "a measure of the knowledge-gain capacity of the OEL processes in the intrinsic
  phase." The authors' own verdict: "this approach is definitely practical, but
  we have to be aware that it does not truly evaluate the OEL property in itself"
  (pp. 13-14). It doubles as the cross-agent comparison method (p. 14), since
  purely autotelic agents otherwise follow incomparable curricula.
- **Novel-goal-rate curve, p. 14.** "measuring the rate of novel goals that the
  agent discovers and/or learns to master throughout its life, and then
  extrapolating from its dynamics... if the number of seen goals asymptotically
  converges to a constant, the agent is not an OEL agent. Otherwise, if the curve
  shows a logarithmic behavior, a linear behavior with positive slope, or even
  better an increasing slope, then the agent is an OEL agent." They note it
  "requires the possibility of marking goals as novel", i.e. it inherits the
  unsolved observer problem.

That is the whole mechanism. Two negative examples, one order distinction, one
curve shape test, one held-out-task protocol borrowed from a 2018 preprint.

## 2. Transferable to Navi-Brain

Nothing here needs training, rollouts, or large context. It is all schema and
SQL. Four things are worth taking, in descending order of value.

**(a) The degenerate-goal filter as an agenda pre-gate — the main steal.**
Cases 1 and 2 (p. 10) are precise names for two failure modes an endogenous
agenda will actually exhibit, and both are cheaply detectable:

- *Case-1 shape (unbounded enumeration):* a candidate goal that is the same
  operation applied to the next element of an enumerable family — "read the next
  paper", "check the log again", "index one more file", "summarise the next
  session". Detect by canonicalising the goal into a **template** (operation
  verb + target type + parameter slot) and asking whether the template has
  already been executed K times with only the parameter slot varying.
- *Case-2 shape (asymptotic refinement):* a candidate goal that is a monotone
  parameter push on an already-measured quantity — "improve the summary",
  "compress memory further", "tighten the threshold". Detect by requiring the
  candidate to name a **measured delta target** and rejecting it when the last M
  attempts on the same metric produced deltas below a floor.

Concretely: a `candidate_goal.degenerate_kind` enum (`null` /
`enumeration` / `asymptotic`) plus a rejecting rule in the agenda's hard-gate
stage, before value-of-computation scoring runs. This is not a scoring term. It
is a filter, because a scoring term can be outvoted and these two shapes are
exactly the ones that score well on a naive novelty component.

**(b) Name the novelty observer, and make it durable — a column, not a search.**
Key definition 1 is undefined until the observer is fixed (p. 8). The natural
lazy implementation for Navi-Brain is "novel = `memory:search` returned no
hits", and that is a bad observer: it is a keyword matcher, so rewording a goal
makes it novel again, and the agenda will novelty-farm against its own
retrieval. Implement instead a `goal_signature` column — a normalised,
canonicalised hash of (operation, target identity, parameter class), computed
deterministically in PHP, never by a worker — with a UNIQUE index and a
first-seen timestamp. Novelty is then `NOT EXISTS(signature)`, an exact,
auditable, non-gameable predicate.

The unbounded-distinct-token proof (p. 8) has a sharp corollary the paper does
not draw: **if the novelty observer's memory is bounded, forgetting manufactures
fake novelty.** A signature table pruned by LRU will make old goals look new and
the agent will loop while its metrics report open-endedness. So the signature
table must live in the durable memory tier and must never be pruned by the same
pressure that prunes episodic content. Cheap degraded version if size ever
matters: keep full-fidelity signatures (they are ~32 bytes each; at Navi-Brain's
thousands-of-records scale this never becomes a problem) and prune the *payload*
instead.

**(c) `goal_space_id` and `goal_origin` as thread/candidate columns.**
Pro4/Pro5 (p. 10) become one column: tag every candidate goal with the goal
space it is drawn from (e.g. `corpus-reading`, `memory-hygiene`,
`desktop-observation`, `self-model-audit`). Then:

- the agenda can see that ten "novel" candidates all sit in one space, which is
  first-order churn;
- the diversity metric becomes *distinct goal spaces touched per week*, not
  *goals generated*, which is far less gameable;
- second-order growth (a genuinely new goal space) becomes an explicit,
  rare, and **authority-gated** event rather than something that happens by
  accident.

Sol6/Sol7/Sol8 (pp. 12-13) become a second column, `goal_origin ∈ {extrinsic,
autotelic, teachable}`: extrinsic = the user or an external event handed it over;
autotelic = the agenda invented it unaided; teachable = the agenda invented it
but under a user steer (a stated preference, a correction, a prior instruction).
This is provenance, so it is directly useful to the authority gate — an
`autotelic` goal proposing an Act-level effect deserves a stricter path than a
`teachable` one derived from an existing grant.

**(d) The novel-goal-rate curve as a stagnation and busywork metric.**
Section 5.1 (p. 14) is a `SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT goal_signature)` bucketed by
week. Flat = not open-ended. The honest use for Navi-Brain is *inverted*: this
curve is a **busywork detector**, not a target. A rising distinct-signature count
with a flat count of *verified outcomes* is the exact signature of Case-1
enumeration. Store both series and diff them. Do not attempt the paper's
log-vs-linear-vs-superlinear curve-shape classification (p. 14); at hundreds of
goals there is no statistical power for that, and the degraded version — "did
distinct signatures increase while verified outcomes did not, over the last 30
days" — is a boolean flag that is actually decidable.

Key definition 2's five-slot list (p. 9) is also worth writing down once as an
audit table: for each of {observation space, action space/options, transition
function, reward function, problems}, which is Navi-Brain permitted to grow?
Perception channels: no, fixed by config. Action space / skill library: yes,
after an observed successful outcome. Transition function (world model): yes,
via memory. Reward/scoring function: **no** — self-modifying the agenda's own
score is the authority-violating slot. Problems/threads: yes, within granted
intentions. That table makes explicit that Navi-Brain is deliberately
open-ended in two slots and deliberately clamped in three.

## 3. Seam

`agenda-voc` (primary — the degenerate-goal filter, `goal_space_id`,
`goal_origin`, first- vs second-order), `metrics-benchmark` (novel-goal-rate
curve, distinct-spaces-per-week, the held-out extrinsic-phase protocol),
`thread-lifecycle` (goal signature and space assigned at nomination),
`memory-tier` (the durable signature table; Sol5 non-forgetting), `authority`
(goal_origin as provenance; second-order goal-space creation as a gated event;
the clamped-slots audit from Key definition 2), `skill-library` (repertoire
growth is the action-space slot).

## 4. Challenge to the current plan

Four real ones, one of them fundamental.

**1. The plan's phrase "controllable learning progress rather than raw novelty"
(ongoing-cognition.md line 345) is not yet implementable, and this paper shows
why.** "Raw novelty" is not a thing you can avoid by resolving to avoid it,
because novelty has no observer-independent meaning (p. 8). Until the observer
is a named deterministic function in the codebase, the agenda's novelty
component means whatever the last retrieval call happened to return. This is a
correction at implementation altitude: the plan states a preference where it
needs a `goal_signature` column and a fixed canonicalisation rule.

**2. Navi-Brain should declare itself deliberately NOT open-ended, and say so in
the doc.** Under Key definition 1 (p. 8), a system that eventually stops
producing new goals is not open-ended. But the plan's own correct behaviour
includes "If no computation has positive estimated value, the correct operation
is sleep with a wake condition" (line 354) — indefinitely, if the world is
quiet. A brain that converges to sleep is, by this paper's definition, a failed
OEL agent. That is the right outcome and the plan should own it explicitly,
because the surrounding literature the plan already cites (line 85, "autotelic
agents and intrinsically motivated goal generation"; records #36/#37) exerts
constant pressure to treat goal-count growth as evidence of cognition. This
paper's Cases 1 and 2 are the anti-target; its Key definition 1 is not a target
at all. Recommended edit: add one line to the agenda section stating that
open-endedness is a diagnostic frame, never an objective, alongside the existing
statement that continuity is an invariant and not a reward.

**3. The paper's definition is completely silent on achievability, and that is
the hole the plan most needs filled.** Nothing in Key definitions 1-3 requires a
goal to be reachable, cheap, or even coherent. A generator that emits an endless
stream of impossible goals satisfies the definition perfectly. The authors
concede the general form of this: "the main limitation of our work is that our
definition of OEL problems does not imply any form of performance progress from
the side of the agent" (p. 14). So the answer to the question this record was
pulled for is blunt: **this paper does not give a criterion for whether a goal is
worth adding to the agenda.** It gives a negative filter against two named
degenerate shapes and a process-level diagnostic over a log. The positive
selection criterion still has to come from #30 (value of computation) and
#36/#37 (controllable learning progress), exactly as the plan already has it.
The plan's existing gate ordering — hard gates, then scored components, then a
verification-probability term (line 348) — is not challenged by this paper; the
verification-probability term is in fact the plan's substitute for the
achievability condition the paper omits, and it is the better design.

**4. Mild but real: the plan may be over-reaching at build step 5.** Sol7/Sol8
plus the remark that "ensuring the OEL property is more difficult for purely
autonomous agents than for teachable agents... being teachable may relax a lot of
the requirements on the goal discovery capabilities of agents" (p. 13) argues
that the affordable target is the *teachable* agent, not the autotelic one.
Navi-Brain has a social partner sitting at the terminal every day and a durable
record of his corrections and preferences. Build step 5, "enable endogenous
agenda selection", should be scoped as **teachable** goal generation — candidates
derived from stored user steers, standing intentions, and observed corrections —
with fully autotelic nomination as a later, narrower, authority-gated case. This
also fixes gap 6 in the audit ("the user is still the practical initiator of
every substantive episode") more cheaply than full autotelic nomination, because
the goal source is already in the database.

Where it agrees: Sol5's lifelong non-forgetting requirement (p. 12) maps
straightforwardly onto the existing memory tiers, supersession, and
`memory:consolidate`; no change needed. The two-phase evaluation protocol
(p. 13) is a weaker version of what the plan already specifies as "downstream
value... compared with a compute-matched prompted control" (lines 522-523).

## 5. Evidence quality

There is no evidence. Zero experiments, zero baselines, zero seeds, zero
ablations, zero datasets, no worked example. This is a position paper in the
literal sense — a genealogy (Section 2, pp. 2-6, with a timeline figure on
p. 5), a set of definitions (Section 3), a taxonomy (Section 4), and two pages
of speculation about evaluation (Section 5). Both evaluation procedures it
discusses are described, not run: the two-phase protocol is cited to Cartoni and
Baldassarre (2018), itself an arXiv preprint, and the goal-rate curve is
proposed with no instance.

**Status: preprint.** The PDF header reads only `arXiv:2311.00344v4 [cs.AI]
7 Jun 2024` (p. 1). No venue stamp, no acceptance line, no reviewer
acknowledgement. Funded by EU Horizon grant 101070381 (PILLAR-robots) (p. 14).
Formatting resembles the TMLR/OpenReview template but carries none of the
published-in markers, so treat it as unreviewed. Four revisions over seven
months suggests it was in review somewhere.

**Is the headline claim supported?** The headline claim is "we provide a
definition", and a definition is not the kind of thing an experiment supports,
so on its own terms it does not fail. But the *implicit* claim — that this
definition is more useful than the prior ones it spends four pages criticising —
is not demonstrated anywhere. The paper never applies its own definition to a
concrete published system to show it discriminates: it never says "POET is
second-order, MineDojo is first-order, Stooke et al. is not open-ended at all."
A definition paper that classifies nothing has not shown its definition does
work.

**Limitations the authors admit:** no implied performance progress (p. 14); the
infinite-horizon requirement conflicts with finite agent lifespans (pp. 9, 13);
the practical test "does not truly evaluate the OEL property in itself" (p. 14);
second-order problems can still be trivial and the needed extra conditions are
not supplied (p. 10); "substantially new" and "interestingly new" are left
undefined (p. 10); abstraction, competence, representational redescription and
creativity are all "left for future work" and may "reveal some deeper
limitations" (p. 14).

**Limitations they do not admit:**

- *Achievability never appears.* An endless stream of impossible goals is
  open-ended under Key definition 1. For any agent that must budget compute this
  is the central omission.
- *Cost never appears.* No definition mentions the price of pursuing a goal.
- *Observer-relativity makes the property unfalsifiable in practice, and they do
  not propose any way to fix an observer.* They identify the problem twice
  (p. 8, p. 14: marking goals as novel "would pose the problems previously
  discussed") and resolve it neither time. Any claim "system X is open-ended" is
  therefore unverifiable without a disclosed novelty function, and the paper does
  not require one to be disclosed.
- *They criticise Abel et al. for defining problems in terms of solutions
  (p. 11), then offer Sol3 — "an open-ended RL agent is an RL agent that solves
  an open-ended RL problem" — which is not circular but is empty.*
- *Case 1 and Case 2 are named and then not fixed.* The whole practical value of
  the paper sits in two sentences on p. 10 whose proposed remedies are both
  admitted to be incomplete on the same page.

## 6. Do not take

- **Do not adopt open-endedness as an objective, a score component, or a health
  metric.** The definition is satisfied by counting to N forever (p. 10). Any
  agenda term rewarding "produced a novel goal" is directly Case-1 farmable.
- **Do not implement Pro5 second-order goal-space generation** (p. 10). No
  mechanism is given, the authors concede it can still be trivial, and letting
  the system invent its own goal *representation spaces* is precisely the move
  the authority model exists to prevent. Tag goal spaces; do not let the agent
  mint them unattended.
- **Do not build any metric that depends on the infinite-horizon condition.**
  Unfalsifiable in finite time and the authors say so (p. 13).
- **Do not implement the log-vs-linear-vs-superlinear curve classification**
  (p. 14). At thousands of records there is no power to distinguish those
  shapes; the fit will be noise dressed as a diagnosis.
- **Do not implement the Cartoni and Baldassarre intrinsic phase as specified**
  (p. 13): "no learning guidance... nor any hardwired knowledge" is both
  impossible and undesirable here. Navi-Brain's authority model, memory, and
  standing intentions are the point. Take only the held-out idea — evaluate
  unattended work against tasks it was not told about.
- **Do not take the frontier-sampling curriculum heuristic** (p. 12, "sampling
  at the frontier of the domain of currently achieved goals") as-is. It is cited
  from RL work that assumes dense goal spaces, cheap resets, and thousands of
  rollouts. None of those exist here.
- **Do not expect a nominator.** The paper contains no procedure for generating
  a candidate goal. It only tells you how to notice that your generator is
  degenerate.
- **Do not let the novelty observer be an LLM judgement.** Observer-relativity
  (p. 8) means whoever defines novelty defines the agent's behaviour; handing
  that to a free-model worker hands it the agenda. Canonicalisation in PHP,
  deterministic, auditable.

## 7. Key citations

- Key definition 1: "An observer considers a process as open-ended if, for any
  time t, there exists a time t' > t at which the process generates a token that
  is new according to this observer's perspective" — novelty is explicitly
  relative to a named observer, which may be "a non-transparent subjective
  evaluator (e.g., a human user) or... an objective mechanism that incorporates
  some measure of novelty (e.g., an algorithm)" (p. 8).
- Open-endedness entails unbounded distinct tokens: for any N there exists
  t' > t with `|Tok_t'| >= |Tok_t| + N` (p. 8). Corollary for a SQLite brain: an
  exact novelty check requires a durable, never-pruned signature store, or
  forgetting will manufacture fake novelty.
- Key definition 2 enumerates the five slots an open-ended process may generate:
  "the observation space, the action space or some hierarchical options, the
  transition function, the reward function or RL problems" (p. 9). Usable as an
  audit of which growth axes Navi-Brain permits and which the authority model
  clamps.
- The two degenerate cases: counting from 1 to N with ever-growing N (Case 1,
  discrete-infinite) and asymptotically approaching a maximum travel speed
  (Case 2, continuous) both satisfy open-endedness "for naive observers who do
  not require a strong notion of novelty" (p. 10). These are the two shapes an
  endogenous agenda must filter out before scoring.
- First- vs second-order: `Pro4` all goals from one space `G`; `Pro5` one process
  generates goal spaces `Omega_j` and a second generates goals within them —
  followed immediately by the admission that "second-order open-ended GCRL
  problems can still generate a trivial diversity of goal spaces" (p. 10).
- Goal-origin taxonomy: `Sol6` extrinsic, `Sol7` autotelic ("fully internal to
  the agent") (p. 12), `Sol8` teachable ("its internal goal generation process
  can be influenced by other agents"), with the assessment that "being teachable
  may relax a lot of the requirements on the goal discovery capabilities of
  agents" (p. 13).
- Practical test: measure the rate of novel goals over life; "if the number of
  seen goals asymptotically converges to a constant, the agent is not an OEL
  agent" — but it "requires the possibility of marking goals as novel, an
  operation which would pose the problems previously discussed" (p. 14).
- Admitted central limitation: "the main limitation of our work is that our
  definition of OEL problems does not imply any form of performance progress
  from the side of the agent" (p. 14). This is why the paper cannot answer
  "is this goal worth pursuing."
