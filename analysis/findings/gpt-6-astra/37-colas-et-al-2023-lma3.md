# Augmenting Autotelic Agents with Large Language Models

**Cédric Colas, Laetitia Teodorescu, Pierre-Yves Oudeyer, Xingdi Yuan, and Marc-Alexandre Côté (2023).** Local version: preprint, arXiv:2305.12487v1, 21 May 2023; no subsequent publication status assumed. [Local PDF](../../../assets/papers/37-colas-et-al-2023-lma3.pdf). Reviewer: **GPT-6 Astra**. Implementation evidence: [shared implementation reference](IMPLEMENTATION.md), snapshot 2026-09-06, HEAD `ee1590009d6fd4c48035c63e15b3896a585757d7`.

The useful contribution is a loop that turns experienced behavior into new goal descriptions, then uses those descriptions to organize further exploration. Navi should experiment with that loop only after separating suggested achievements from verified achievements. This paper demonstrates substantial goal-description discovery in a small deterministic world; it does not establish reliable open-ended skill acquisition in changing environments.

**Core idea and mechanism.** An autotelic agent generates the goals it practices. LMA3 supplies three language-model functions around a deliberately simple learner: retrospective trajectory relabeling, prospective goal generation, and achievement evaluation. Language supplies reusable categories and combinations beyond the environment's command vocabulary; pretraining is interpreted as an imperfect channel of cultural transmission (§§1–3, PDF pp. 1–6, Figs. 1–2).

CookingWorld provides textual observations and approximately 30–50 currently admissible actions from 143 possibilities. Episodes last 25 steps and restart in the same deterministic environment. This eliminates much of the grounding and control problem: the environment supplies legal commands, and successful sequences can be replayed from a fixed start (§3.1, p. 4).

The first episode uses random actions. For 4,000 bootstrap episodes, goals are sampled uniformly from discovered descriptions accepted by the reward model. Subsequently, the goal generator sees the previous trajectory and up to 60 achieved goals, and proposes a high-level goal decomposed into two to four known subgoals. After each rollout, the relabeler proposes up to ten incidental achievements and their first-completion steps. A separate reward prompt evaluates the intended goal, subgoals, and retrospective labels (§3.2, pp. 5–6).

The policy is a dictionary mapping goal descriptions to action sequences. Each retrospective achievement yields a prefix from the episode's beginning to the reported completion step; only the shortest known sequence for that description is retained. Subgoal sequences are concatenated. Exploration includes probabilistic truncation of the final subgoal sequence, with ε = 0.2, followed by admissible actions sampled inversely to their occurrence. Calling this an evolutionary learner should not suggest a trained neural goal-conditioned controller: its principal adaptation is storing and shortening sequences (§3.2, p. 6).

All modules use `gpt-3.5-turbo-0301` in the experiments; Appendix B gives temperature 0 for reward judgments and 0.9 for generation/relabeling. Two demonstration trajectories and example reasoning support the prompts. Thus “no predefined goal representations” means no engineer-enumerated target space and goal-specific reward functions supplied to LMA3. It does not mean learning without human examples, engineered environment semantics, or extensive pretrained knowledge.

**Evidence and limitations.** Five seeds per condition compare full LMA3, three ablations, and a hardcoded oracle. The oracle uses 69 predefined goals and exact relabeling. The ablations remove human tips; tips plus the goal generator; or tips plus chain-of-thought prompting. They are informative interventions, but not a full factorial design: there is no generator-removed condition retaining the full system's human tips (§4, p. 6).

The strongest independent outcome is success on the 69 human-defined goals, evaluated with hardcoded reward functions that LMA3 did not receive during training. Figure 3a shows broad coverage, with the oracle better and the full system not best among the language-model variants. An additional oracle sweep through stored trajectories raises full LMA3 to **0.98 ± 0.020**, the no-tips variant to **0.99 ± 0.007**, and the no-generator/no-tips variant to **0.98 ± 0.000** (Fig. 3b, p. 7; mean ± standard deviation). This requires no further environment interaction. What the paper calls finetuning here is retrospective oracle relabeling, not parameter training. It shows that useful behavior can already exist in memory while its goal indexing remains incomplete; it does not show that the autonomous relabeler itself achieved near-perfect recognition.

LMA3 discovers approximately **9,000 distinct goal redescriptions in 10,000 episodes** (§4, p. 7). Stem richness, exponential stem entropy, and a stem h-index supplement raw counts; conjunction/category-word frequencies and SentenceBERT distances assess abstraction and uniqueness (Figs. 4–6, pp. 8–9). These are principally linguistic measures. Synonyms can inflate diversity, while one verb can conceal very different behavior. Nine thousand descriptions therefore do not imply nine thousand independent control competencies. Prompting with abstract examples substantially increases these measures; the authors describe the goal generator's additional diversity effect as slight. They also report that its complex goals largely remain recipe variations (p. 9).

Mastery on three samples of 200 goals per seed—own discovered goals, own unique goals, and other agents' unique goals—is assessed by the LM reward function (Fig. 7a, p. 10). The latter evaluates new descriptions in the same world, not transfer to new environments. These outcomes lack the oracle independence of Figure 3. A human-labeled check of 100 trajectories, comprising 56 true successes and 44 failures, gives approximately **9% false negatives and 9% false positives** (Fig. 7b). This is useful disclosure, but a small aggregate check cannot establish reliability for rare abstractions or temporal predicates. Separate prompts using the same model and related examples can make correlated mistakes; two passes do not justify multiplying their error rates as if independent.

The local appendix contains concrete defects relevant to that concern. In B.1.1, p. 16, the demonstration cuts a potato at step 85 but labels “cut a yellow potato” complete at step 84. B.1.3, p. 18, similarly labels “find a knife and use it to cut a potato” at 84, despite its accompanying reasoning identifying 85. Another full-system label says “cook an onion first then cut a bell pepper,” although the displayed trajectory cuts the pepper at 52 and cooks the onion at 53 (pp. 15, 18). These are visible in the PDF, not extraction artifacts. They do not prove which mistakes survived the experimental reward check. They do show that the published demonstrations are unsuitable as unquestioned timestamp or temporal-order supervision. Under shortest-prefix retention, an accepted premature timestamp could preferentially preserve an insufficient sequence.

The authors explicitly reserve robust policy learning, performance/progress-driven relabeling, and larger environments for future work (§5, pp. 10–11). The claim of cultural transmission is an architectural interpretation of pretrained language influence, not a measured process of reciprocal human–agent cultural evolution. Historical cost is material: approximately 550,000 calls at 4,000 tokens each, reported as $4,400 overall and about $240 per full-system seed. These are the paper's contemporary costs, not current pricing or a Navi budget estimate.

**Prioritized recommendations.** These are proposed experiments and acceptance criteria, not changes or experiments performed during this review.

1. **P1 — Establish trustworthy achievement labels before introducing a curriculum.** Build an isolated relabeling experiment over normalized `ActionTrace`/`ActionExecution`/`ProcedureRun` evidence, preserving observed outcomes, event order and immutable procedure generations (Q29). Each proposed achievement should carry a predicate, supporting event IDs, earliest sufficient prefix, verifier identity and an explicit unknown state. Use bounded task predicates or independent human judgments to assess proposals; the generating model's agreement is insufficient. A later completion-path integration would require equivalent evidence checks at `integrateCompletionCheck`, with source-validity patterns borrowed from consolidation. Dependencies are trajectory normalization and independent labels, including failed actions, reversed order, missing evidence and incomplete prefixes. Proposed gate: held-out accepted-label precision at least 98%, recall at least 80%, and premature completion below 1%, with uncertainty intervals and temporal-goal subgroup reporting. Failure on any gate blocks automatic promotion into reusable achievements. This directly targets the paper's measured reward errors and appendix defects.

2. **P2 — Compare retrospective discovery with prospective goal generation at equal cost.** Once P1 succeeds, maintain a separate experimental catalog containing achieved predicate, language aliases, evidence, valid starting conditions and associated procedure generation. A bounded proposer can consume that catalog and produce two-to-four-step candidates for an already authorized practice task; `createIntention` and `ProceduralMemory::compose` remain eventual integration boundaries, not automatic publication destinations. Compare a fixed curriculum, retrospective relabeling plus uniform sampling, and relabeling plus LM composition, holding prompts, learner and inference/environment budgets constant. Measure independently verified distinct behaviors and success on held-out task predicates per token and interaction, collapsing paraphrases. Proposed adoption gate: composition improves verified coverage per budget by at least 10% over relabeling alone without lowering held-out success by more than two percentage points. Failure favors the cheaper retrospective system. This comparison follows the oracle-relabeling gain and modest generator ablation effect more closely than immediately building the entire architecture.

3. **P3 — Require evidence that composed procedures work from intermediate states.** A sequence successful from an episode reset need not work after another sequence changes the world. For experiments using `ProceduralMemory::compose:983` and `advanceRun:1221`, attach explicit entry predicates and verify each subgoal outcome before continuing. Preserve existing generation/adapter guards; add a bounded abort or replanning decision when the observed state violates the next entry condition. This is a transfer-oriented extension, not a mechanism demonstrated by LMA3. It costs state capture, predicate maintenance and additional checks. Compare guarded composition with sequence concatenation across changed initial conditions and controlled intervening state changes. Reject the extension if it fails to reduce invalid continuations or if added inference cost eliminates its verified-success-per-budget advantage. Never infer generalization from replay success at the original reset alone.

The principal adoption risk is a reinforcing loop in which fluent descriptions become achievement labels, then “mastered” subskills, then premises for more ambitious goals. A second model prompt can reduce some mistakes without breaking that dependence. Linguistic novelty also need not match Aku's priorities; relevance must be evaluated against useful task outcomes. Learned competence-progress allocation is promising but explicitly future work here and absent in the master's inspected Navi routes, so it should not be credited to either system on this evidence.

**Verdict: experiment.** Prioritize independently verified retrospective achievement discovery; expand goal generation only if it adds behavioral coverage at matched cost. Preserve Navi's existing typed procedures and provenance, while treating generalized goal labels as proposals until their evidence is checked.
