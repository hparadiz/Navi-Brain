**Central judgment.** Experiment with the paper's active system-identification loop for narrow, observable capability failures. Its contribution is a causal chain: competing models predict different consequences of an action; the system performs a discriminating action; measured consequences revise the models; the revised model supports useful behavior. Navi already has several necessary components, but a record called a self-model and an adapter method called simulation do not establish that chain.

**Mechanism and assumptions.** The robot has eight motorized joints, eight joint-angle sensors and two body-tilt sensors. Joint sensors verify attainment of commanded angles; body orientation supplies the modeling observations. The initial hypothesis space contains planar arrangements of eight limbs, including chains and trees. Thus, learning starts without the correct arrangement, but with considerable mechanical and representational structure supplied by the designers (PDF p. 3, experimental-system paragraphs).

After an initial arbitrary action, stochastic optimization produces **15 candidate self-models** fitting accumulated action–sensor observations. A separate search chooses an actuation pattern maximizing disagreement among their predicted sensor signals. The physical robot executes it, and all accumulated observations constrain further modeling. After **16 cycles**, behavior optimization uses a selected model to generate locomotion; that behavior then transfers to the robot (PDF p. 3, Fig. 1).

In reviewer notation, this is roughly `choose a maximizing dispersion of {prediction(M_i, a)}`, followed by fitting the models to the newly observed outcome. This describes the mechanism, not an equation supplied by the article. Disagreement is a proxy for informative intervention; the paper does not establish a calibrated Bayesian posterior or exact expected information gain. The models must contain plausible alternatives and disagree on available actions. Shared model error or indistinguishable alternatives defeats that proxy.

The explicit simulator supplies a particularly important bridge: training uses orientation, while behavior search rewards predicted displacement. A generic predictor of tilt alone cannot provide a locomotion objective. The simulator's prior structure enables predictions about an outcome absent from the identification signal (PDF p. 3, discussion of alternative representations). Transferring this insight to software requires an executable model connecting diagnostic observations to task consequences, not merely better prose about system health.

“Continuous” describes repeated modeling, exploration and behavior generation. The demonstrated protocol uses bounded phases. Unexpected sensor–motor patterns **or an external signal** may restart modeling; the main article supplies no quantified detection-latency or false-alarm result. Furthermore, after damage, **topology is fixed to the previously inferred morphology and limb sizes may scale** (PDF p. 3). Initial topology discovery and subsequent parameter adaptation are demonstrated; unconstrained topology rediscovery after arbitrary damage is not.

**Evidence and its limits.** There are 30 optimization experiments per algorithm before damage and another 30 per algorithm afterward. All use 16 physical actions, with approximately comparable simulation budgets. Baseline 1 collects random actions before batch modeling; baseline 2 alternates modeling with random actions; the proposed method alternates modeling with model-selected actions (PDF pp. 3–5, Table 1).

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| Initial model error, mean ± SD | 9.62 ± 1.47 cm | 9.70 ± 1.45 cm | 7.31 ± 1.22 cm |

The post-damage reference length is **9.7 cm**. Initial and post-damage error rows have different interpretations and should not be combined into a single longitudinal accuracy curve. Mean model evaluations range from about 247,000–262,000 before damage and 278,000–296,000 afterward. The physical-action budget is small because considerable search occurs internally; this is not evidence of small total computation or short elapsed recovery time.

The comparisons support choosing informative actions within this model family. However, a 43.3% topology success rate leaves **17 of 30 runs unsuccessful**. The article gives no significance test for the success-rate contrasts. Baselines 1 and 2 performing similarly does not establish that iterative fitting is generally useless; it establishes little advantage here when intervention selection remains random.

Across 30 model-driven runs, disagreement correlates with actual model error: **Spearman ρ = 0.425, P < 0.02** (PDF p. 4). This moderate association supports a heuristic ranking signal, not a correctness certificate. Agreement can result from shared misspecification or premature convergence.

The locomotion evaluation uses the best model from the run selected for greatest agreement among those 30 runs. Behavior optimization runs 30 times on that model; each resulting gait executes physically, alongside 30 random gaits. Fig. 3 shows a forward-motion advantage but also a substantial difference between predicted and realized displacement. Its error bars are **two standard deviations**, not confidence intervals. Authors attribute transfer discrepancies plausibly to friction and kinematic bifurcations; these causes are not separately isolated (PDF pp. 4–5).

That selection matters: the gait trials are not 30 independently successful complete learn-and-walk pipelines. Post-damage modeling also starts from the selected pre-damage model, so its results are conditional on a favorable initialization. The damaged gait shown in Fig. 2 supports feasibility, but the supplied main article provides no analogous aggregate post-damage locomotion comparison. Multiple body designs, repeated damage histories, sensor failures and environmental changes are not established by these experiments. Claims about animal cognition and higher machine cognition remain proposals, not measured outcomes (PDF pp. 4–5, concluding discussion).

**Mapping to Navi-Brain.** The useful transfer is at the executive's operational capability model: predicting how tools, sensors or execution routes behave under changed conditions. It is not a claim about phenomenal self-awareness or a prescription to imitate robot morphology in a language model.

**Prioritized recommendations.** This software transfer is **speculative synthesis**. The following are proposed experiments and engineering gates, not work performed or thresholds derived from the paper.

**P1 — Build a small operational hypothesis experiment offline.** Start with one observable failure domain, such as an emulated tool whose output becomes unavailable, stale or schema-incompatible. Represent a few distinct mechanisms with typed predictions for each permitted diagnostic observation. Seal predictions before outcomes, then update fit from independently recorded results. Reuse the design of `OtherModel::sealPredictions/resolvePredictions` and `ExecutiveCore::finishAction`; retain contextual conditions and evidence IDs rather than immediately promoting a global SelfModelFact (I05, I07, I09).

This requires an outcome schema, controlled fault scenarios and cheap executable predictors. A dozen paraphrases from the same model would not establish hypothesis diversity. Evaluate held-out fault scenarios with proper predictive scores against persistence and a single fitted model. Fail this stage if the ensemble cannot distinguish the planted causes, does not improve held-out prediction, or converges confidently when every candidate is wrong. Include a deliberately omitted fault type to measure rejection of an inadequate hypothesis space. This is the paper's identification problem made concrete before changing the executive.

**P2 — Add budgeted action selection only if P1 succeeds.** In an isolated prototype beside `DecisionStateMachine::integrate`, rank already permitted diagnostic candidates by noise-normalized predictive disagreement, with explicit execution-cost and latency budgets. Retain `ProceduralMemory::inspectCandidate` and installed source/adapter restrictions (I05, Q03). The new selector should identify a particular discriminating observation, not reward generic “look” frequency or surprise after the fact. Prediction evaluation costs grow with hypotheses × candidate actions; small deterministic models are preferable for the first experiment.

Reproduce the paper's two controls: batch random acquisition and alternating random acquisition, with equal probe and model-computation budgets. Add a single-model selector ablation. Across at least 30 held-out fault instances per condition, record diagnosis accuracy, probe count, elapsed time and computation. A provisional adoption gate is at least **25% fewer probes** than alternating random at matched accuracy, with false diagnoses at most **5%** and a paired uncertainty interval excluding no improvement. Failure includes exhausted budgets without benefit or savings that disappear when latency and computation are counted. Choose these operating limits before evaluating.

**P3 — Make identified change alter subsequent behavior.** After successful prediction and selection experiments, add explicit versioned dependencies from affected capability claims to the procedures that rely on them. Resolve adverse observations through `finishAction` and the existing typed-failure invalidation mechanism; use `setSelfModelFact` to publish supported revisions with their scope and evidence. Q07 establishes that this adds a missing causal connection while retaining the existing context consumers and direct action-failure adaptation (I07, I08, Q06–Q07).

Use persistent residual error as a trigger to reconsider the model family, even when all candidates agree. Apply a bounded threshold for transient noise and prevent stale predictions from restoring a superseded capability. Dependencies and repeated observations add storage and recovery latency. Evaluate complete tasks after injected changes, comparing frozen facts, passive revisions, and revisions connected to dispatch. Fail if recorded “belief revisions” increase without reducing repeated failed actions, if obsolete procedures remain eligible after acknowledged invalidation, or if noise causes more than 5% false capability withdrawals. I10's existing revision/recovery counters cannot substitute for these independent task outcomes.

**Verdict: experiment, with P1 as the next step.** This paper gives credible physical evidence that actively distinguishing competing models can improve identification and enable useful behavior transfer under constrained damage. It does not demonstrate generally reliable autonomous recovery, calibrated self-knowledge, or consciousness. Navi's most consequential opportunity is to make a narrow self-model earn its status through sealed action predictions, discriminating observations and measurable changes in successful behavior; broader production adoption should await that demonstration.
