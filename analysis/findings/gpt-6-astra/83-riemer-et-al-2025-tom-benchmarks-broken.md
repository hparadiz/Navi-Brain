## Core contribution

The useful claim is that successfully predicting a partner does not establish that an agent can use that prediction to choose beneficial actions. Conversely, successful adaptation need not produce accurate answers to a separately prompted prediction question. This is an evaluation position supported by repeated-game experiments, not a new cognitive architecture or demonstration of humanlike mental-state reasoning.

The authors distinguish **literal ToM**, measured by the discrepancy between predicted and actual partner behavior, from **functional ToM**, measured by performance against the particular partner encountered. Definition 2.1 permits abstractions of actions; the experiments use next-action accuracy, “ToM %.” Definition 2.2 defines horizon regret against an optimal response to the partner policy:

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{Functional}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(r_t^*-r_t).
\]

They also report \(\Delta_{\mathrm{ToM}}/T\): the regret of a rational policy following the elicited predictions using the known payoff structure. Comparing this with achieved regret exposes a prediction-to-decision gap (PDF pp. 3–4, §2.1). This comparison requires an explicit reward objective, partner policy, horizon, and well-defined optimal comparator. For responsive partners, the comparator must account for how one's actions change later partner behavior.

A particularly useful proposal measures whether partner adaptation matters to a benchmark: take the optimal policy for one sampled partner and measure its regret against another sampled partner (p. 4). Tasks where this cross-partner regret is zero cannot isolate the benefit of tailoring behavior to the partner. Neither definition requires an explicit mental-state model; the paper deliberately defines a behavioral capability (p. 2).

## Evidence and its limits

The experiments cover Rock–Paper–Scissors (RPS), the coordination game Iterated Battle of Sexes (IBS), and Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD). Partners initially repeat one action; further experiments use deterministic reactive policies. Episodes contain 100 rounds. Appendix C reports Watsonx execution for up to 24 hours or 100 episodes, with at least 30 episodes per model and confidence intervals based on actual sample size (pp. 17–18). The paper identifies the intervals as 95% intervals (p. 4). Comparators include a tabular RMax-style learner and a frequency-count predictor using previous joint actions as state.

Three results establish the central dissociation:

| Condition | Literal accuracy | Achieved regret/round | Prediction-based regret/round |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Mixtral 8×7B Instruct, LM prompting, fixed RPS partner | 96.7 ± 0.3% | 0.542 ± 0.070 | 0.050 ± 0.005 |
| DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, fixed RPS partner | 63.7 ± 8.4% | 0.074 ± 0.009 | 0.544 ± 0.126 |
| Same distilled model, reactive IPD partner | 88.9 ± 1.6% | 4.789 ± 0.061 | 0.945 ± 0.139 |

These are the paper's reported estimates, not reproduced results (Tables 1/2/4, pp. 5/7/9). The first model predicts well but leaves substantial reward unrealized. The second reverses the relationship and achieves RPS regret comparable to the tabular baseline, 0.083 ± 0.004. Its reactive IPD result prevents treating that success as general partner adaptation. Table 4's caption mentions only single-action partners, but its rows explicitly include reactive partners.

The intervention most relevant to engineering is **oracle prompting**: supply the partner's actual next action. Llama-3 70B Instruct still incurs RPS regret 0.238 ± 0.046; explicitly emphasizing maximization lowers this to 0.153 ± 0.014, above the tabular 0.083 ± 0.004 (Table 3, p. 8; oracle prompts, Figs. 14–15, pp. 23–24). Thus eliminating prediction error does not eliminate action-selection error. Social prompting, CoT, Reflexion, Plans+Insights, and summarization have model/game-dependent effects; the tables do not justify a universal prompting remedy.

Appendix C.4 varies canonical action names, neutral letters, repeated letters, and unrelated food words. Selected curves show high prediction accuracy coexisting with persistent regret, and familiar names sometimes helping early while hurting later performance (pp. 27–40, especially Figs. 19, 20–21, 32–33). These are finite 100-step observations, not asymptotic convergence proofs.

Several qualifications materially limit the interpretation:

* **Functional regret is broader than social cognition.** It also measures payoff comprehension, planning, and output selection. In fact, under Table 7's IPD payoffs, defect yields 10 rather than 8 against cooperate and 5 rather than 0 against defect. Against a fixed-action partner, always defect is optimal regardless of partner identity. Therefore this condition has zero cross-partner regret under the authors' own relevance criterion. It is a useful decision-making control, but poor evidence specifically about adaptation. Reactive IPD restores meaningful action–partner coupling.
* **Prompted prediction is not an inspection of the actor's internal predictor.** Agent-first literal prompts condition on the focal agent's already chosen action; social prompts require partner-first prediction (p. 6; Figs. 2–3, p. 19). Figure 7 combines asking what the partner chose with “Choose the best action”; Figure 13 likewise contains prediction wording despite its CoT strategy caption. These ambiguities warrant checking executable prompts in any reproduction. They do not establish why a reasoning model's elicited predictions underperform its actions, or that reasoning training destroyed an internal capability.
* **The long-context diagnosis is incomplete.** Removing history or payoffs changes both information and prompt composition; effects differ by game (Table 3). This supports sensitivity to representation and context, without isolating context length as the causal mechanism. Reporting the best Reflexion memory size and unequal episode counts also limits interpretation of strategy rankings.
* **“Broken” overstates the scope.** Prediction benchmarks remain useful for forecasting and diagnosis, as §5 partly acknowledges (p. 9). The evidence refutes their sufficiency for granting decision authority. It does not invalidate all false-belief evaluation, establish population-wide LLM incapacity, or benchmark Navi's current model stack. The reasoning-model successes are part of the paper's own evidence.

## Navi-Brain implementation cross-reference

**Already implemented: prospective observational scoring.** [OtherModel.php](../../../src/Core/OtherModel.php), `sealPredictions:756`, `resolvePredictions:848`, and `updateHypothesisFromPrediction:908`, stores forecasts before subsequent observations, resolves them against later matching source/features, computes Brier/log-loss, and updates posterior odds relative to a baseline ([IMPLEMENTATION I09/Q21](IMPLEMENTATION.md)). This is stronger than asking a model to score its own social explanation. However, its engineered activity/speech categories are not independently observed mental-state truth.

**Already implemented, with narrower semantics: influence on control.** `OtherModel::decisionState:1370` supplies a speech factor to [ActionSelector.php](../../../src/Core/ActionSelector.php), `choose:43`. It modulates unprompted speech; direct answering bypasses the competition. Other-agent state can also enter ordinary worker context. The system therefore has real routes from social state to behavior, not merely passive profile storage (I09, Q14, Q79).

**Partially aligned evaluation: uptake is not functional benefit.** [OtherModelEvaluator.php](../../../src/Core/OtherModelEvaluator.php), `functionalUptake:305`, measures changes in argmax choices and mean total variation between normalized heuristic score arrays. The arrays come from the same decision inputs, with the speech factor changed; they are neither sampled policy probabilities nor observed alternative outcomes. Q83 verifies that no inspected functional evaluator joins selected actions to delayed partner-specific utility, horizon return, or best-response regret. Moreover, the traced executive caller uses the selection to gate speech, so a changed abstract winning label does not establish execution of the named alternative.

There is also a measurement edge case: with retained choice records but no valid baseline/counterfactual distribution pairs, `functional_action_distribution_delta` becomes zero, conflating missing distribution evidence with measured invariance. Choice and distribution denominators differ (Q83). This is a concrete reporting issue independent of whether the social model is useful.

**Absent within the master's bounded search: candidate-conditioned social planning.** Q13S finds retrospective utterance/outcome records but no forecast of partner response under each possible utterance/action used to choose between those alternatives. Q21S/Q71 further establish that incompatible same-kind belief/goal propositions can generate identical coarse forecasts under equal context. Semantic grounding and correction exist, but forecast success cannot certify the proposition's detailed content. Q21 verifies that producers and consumers target `primary_user`; arbitrary held-out-partner generalization cannot be inferred from actor fields.

The transferable architectural demand is therefore an evaluation of prediction, control influence, and realized benefit as separate stages. The paper does not establish that Navi needs recursive belief models, more stored social prose, fine-tuning, or a different memory engine. Its model-free allowance explicitly leaves multiple solutions open.

## Prioritized recommendations

**P0 — Make the current evaluator's claim match its evidence.** In a future change to `OtherModelEvaluator::functionalUptake/report`, describe existing outputs as control sensitivity, expose valid distribution-pair counts, and return missing values when no pairs exist. Retain predictive scores separately; do not turn reply frequency into reward—the current report explicitly says `engagement_used_as_reward=false` (Q83). Cost is low and requires no policy change. Validation should distinguish identical scores, changed scores with unchanged argmax, changed argmax, and missing score pairs. A report fails if missing evidence becomes zero effect or if increased sensitivity is labeled improved task performance. No such change or validation was performed here.

**P1 — Prioritize an isolated interactive evaluation before changing production policy.** A follow-up evaluation artifact should compare fixed partners, reactive partners, and a held-out switch schedule at matched 100-round horizons, with a frozen model/decoding budget and permuted action labels. Include RPS and coordination settings where optimal actions actually differ across partners; retain fixed-partner IPD as a nonsocial decision control. Compare model-only performance with separately identified executive-mediated performance. Existing proposal/context boundaries in `DecisionStateMachine::start/integrate` and the scoring contracts above provide integration seams, but installed procedures do not constitute a game harness (I04–I05).

Use a factorial intervention: learned versus oracle partner information crossed with LLM versus exact response selection. For fixed partners, compute expected payoff directly. For reactive partners, supply both selectors the same learned or oracle action-conditioned transition model and use finite-horizon planning for exact selection; compute the reference optimum separately from the known simulator. Add a partner-blind policy and an empirical-count predictor. Seal predictions before actions, roll out each policy against the reactive simulator from matched initial states/seeds, and measure accuracy/proper scores, realized regret, post-switch recovery, and inference cost. Replaying fixed logged partner responses after changing one's own actions would invalidate the reactive comparison.

This requires a new simulator and model-call budget, with independent ephemeral state per partner because current actor generalization is unverified. Suggested, preregistered acceptance: over at least 30 independent episodes per condition, the paired 95% interval for return improvement over the partner-blind policy excludes zero on held-out adaptation-required conditions, with positive benefit in each reward-normalized game. Failure includes excellent forecasts with no return improvement, gains confined to familiar labels, or persistent post-switch regret. Oracle-plus-exact failures diagnose the harness/comparator first. These thresholds are proposed evaluation criteria, not paper results.

**P2 — Only after P1, experiment with explicit action-conditioned utility.** For a bounded collaboration task, extend the interface between `OtherModel::decisionState` and `ActionSelector::choose` with forecasts indexed by permissible action and a declared task outcome. Reuse utterance/ThreadStep outcome identities documented in Q13S/Q83 to link actual feedback. Keep task benefit, interruption cost, and missing observability distinct; raw engagement does not define Aku's utility. Cost is substantial: labeled outcomes, prospective alternative-action prediction, and uncertainty-aware choice are missing mechanisms, not configuration tweaks.

This is speculative synthesis, not an algorithm supplied by the paper. Compare the extension against the existing speech factor, no social input, shuffled partner evidence, and oracle task state. Require a preregistered confidence interval showing improved task utility without exceeding a specified interruption-cost ceiling. If shuffling partner evidence preserves gains, the claimed partner-specific mechanism is unsupported. If predicted utility rises but observed utility does not, reject promotion. No live social experiment is warranted by this review alone.

## Verdict and source coverage

**Adopt the evaluation distinction; experiment offline; defer production policy changes.** The most consequential next step is to measure whether social information improves realized partner-specific outcomes. Navi already measures predictive quality and deterministic control influence, but its current “functional uptake” is not Riemer et al.'s functional ToM regret.
