**Central finding.** The useful contribution is a causal requirement for mental-state inference: a hypothesized belief must affect predicted action through its content, jointly with the agent's desires and information access. Navi already has substantive hypothesis storage and delayed predictive scoring. However, the master's Q21S establishes that incompatible belief propositions can receive identical speech forecasts and identical evidence multipliers. Those scores cannot discriminate the propositions' semantic truth. This paper supports an isolated experiment addressing that gap; it does not justify replacing the executive with a general POMDP solver.

**Mechanism and assumptions.** BToM models an observer inferring another agent's subjective beliefs and rewards by inverting a partially observable Markov decision process. The observer knows the environmental configuration and sees the agent's physical trajectory; the agent has only the observations afforded by its location. Crucially, the agent's distribution over possible worlds is distinct from the observer's uncertainty about that distribution (pp. 2–4, “Computational Framework,” Fig. 1).

In the food-truck domain, physical states are grid locations; environmental states assign Korean, Lebanese, and Mexican trucks to two parking spots. Actions include four movement directions, staying, and eating. Actions cost one unit, truck-specific eating rewards represent preference strength, and costs/rewards cease after eating. Movement can fail with probability ε. Visual access is a 360-degree isovist constrained by buildings, with probability ν of an ambiguous observation, interpreted as failing to notice visible information (p. 3, “Formal modeling”).

For the static environment, the agent updates its belief according to

\[
b_t(y)\propto P(o_t\mid x_t,y)b_{t-1}(y).
\]

Point-based value iteration approximates optimal POMDP values on a discretized belief space. The action likelihood is a softmax of the lookahead state-action value, proportional to \(\exp[\beta Q^{LA}(b,x,y,a)]\); β controls policy determinism. Thus an information-seeking detour can be rational because learning which truck is available improves the eventual reward–travel-cost tradeoff. This is instrumental information value, not a separately implemented curiosity reward (pp. 2–3).

The observer combines prior mental-state hypotheses with trajectory likelihoods, accounting for latent observations and resulting belief changes. Its target is \(P(b_t,r\mid x_{1:T},y)\), with \(T\geq t\), analogous to forward–backward inference. Later behavior can revise an attribution of an earlier belief. In fact, the experiment asks retrospectively about the agent's **initial** belief. This smoothing task should not be reported as a demonstration of online next-action prediction (p. 4).

Beliefs and rewards are discretized at resolution seven; truck rewards range over −20, 0, …, 100, calibrated to the environments' spatial scale. Predictions use posterior expected rewards and beliefs. Separate belief and desire estimates lose potentially important dependence: strongly wanting an unlikely reward and mildly wanting a likely reward can explain similar behavior. The model carries joint uncertainty before producing those marginal summaries (pp. 1, 4).

Figure 2's diagnostic example is stronger than simply inferring the destination as the goal. The student passes visible K, inspects an initially hidden spot containing L, then returns to K. Under the assumed costs and stationary preferences, this supports preferring the absent M over both available alternatives. However, the prose's “only consistent desire” language is stronger than the stochastic formulation warrants: alternative hypotheses can retain likelihood under noisy action or perception, and omitted motives can explain the trajectory outside this model class (p. 3).

**Evidence and its limits.** The experiment recruited 17 MIT-pool participants and excluded one who misunderstood instructions, leaving 16. Starting position, path, judgment point, building layout, and truck configuration were varied; invalid combinations were removed, leaving 78 trials. Labels and spatial reflections were randomized, and names/colors varied to discourage carrying beliefs or preferences across students. Subjects received an explanation of visibility, which remained displayed during trials. Ratings used seven-point scales; belief ratings were normalized for the plotted comparisons (pp. 4–6, Figs. 3–4).

Only the 54 ending-point trials entered the reported analysis: debriefing and variability indicated confusion about midpoint judgments. The comparison models were **TrueBel**, which assumes full knowledge of the world, and **NoObs**, which allows an initial subjective belief but never updates it from observations. Figure 3 reports correlations against mean human judgments:

| Attribution | BToM | TrueBel | NoObs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Desire | 0.90 | 0.67 | 0.61 |
| Initial belief | 0.76 | 0.11 | 0.39 |

BToM's displayed fit uses β = 1.0 and ν = 0.1. Parameters were selected from a coarse grid; only β applies to the alternatives. Figure 4 supplies qualitative checks involving detour length, inspecting an unseen option, returning to a known alternative, and inferring preference for an absent truck. Five constructed trials were designated “irrational” because a shorter inspection route dominated regardless of initial belief or desire; irrational-case points account for many large residuals (pp. 4–6).

These are persuasive within-domain comparisons, with several qualifications:

- Correlation of participant means measures agreement with aggregate judgments, not recovery of independently measured real agents' private mental states. Aggregation also obscures individual variability; correlation does not establish probability calibration.
- The reported fits are not held-out predictions. There are no reported confidence intervals for the correlation differences, cross-validation, or quantified significance tests accompanying the claim that alternatives fit “significantly worse.” The additional noise parameter and prior/discretization sensitivity remain relevant uncertainties.
- Excluding midpoint trials after observing confusion narrows the conclusion to comprehensible completed trajectories. The paper does not establish the full time course of human inference.
- Assuming full observability or removing belief updating worsens the fits, supporting these representational distinctions within the tested model family. This does not prove that people literally solve POMDPs, or exclude alternative heuristics and learned predictors.
- This is an ideal-observer analysis with a supplied ontology, dynamics, visibility model, and approximately rational policy. It reports no computational scaling benchmark. The six-page account leaves replication details such as numerical tolerances and explicit prior mass assignments underspecified.

The authors explicitly leave separate instantaneous goals and explicit subgoal sequences to future work (pp. 5–6, “Conclusion & Future Work”). Neither an intention hierarchy nor recursive multi-agent reasoning is demonstrated. The poorer fit on dominated routes is especially relevant to engineering: computational limitations, habits, forgotten information, and unmodeled objectives should not automatically become exotic inferred preferences.

**Prioritized recommendations.** The following are proposed evaluations and changes, not experiments performed during this review.

1. **P0 — Make proposition validation depend on discriminating evidence.** At OtherModel::updateHypothesisFromPrediction and OtherModelEvaluator::report, distinguish coarse forecast quality from semantic proposition credence. Retain the existing speech forecasts, but do not report their success as proposition validation without a content-sensitive likelihood. Define paired fixtures with identical kind/context and opposed beliefs about a task fact; hold desires and action costs fixed and supply an opportunity whose predicted choice differs under those beliefs. Include nondiagnostic actions where uncertainty should remain. Dependencies are explicit fact/action labels and independently specified outcomes; this does not require a full planner. **Failure criterion:** the proposed semantic scorer still assigns identical action distributions to deliberately diagnostic opposed hypotheses, marks a proposition semantically validated solely from nondiscriminating speech success, or cannot outperform a kind-only baseline on held-out diagnostic cases. Preserve current coarse forecast scores as a separate comparison.

2. **P1 — Represent the modeled person's information access explicitly.** Add an isolated bounded task-state projection beside OtherModel and [AgentObservationProjector](../../../src/Perception/AgentObservationProjector.php), using observation ingestion at OtherModel::observeReading/runObservation. Keep Navi's known task state separate from primary_user's hypothesized belief distribution. Record time, fact, evidence ID, and whether access is observed, reported, or uncertain. Presence alone must not imply receipt or comprehension. This transfers the paper's observation model; extending it to changing task facts is an engineering extrapolation beyond its static truck configurations. Cost lies in defining the task ontology and reliably establishing exposure; existing records do not guarantee complete observation capture (Q53). **Validation:** pair identical worlds with different access histories and identical histories with hidden world changes. An unseen change with no informative cue must leave the actor-belief estimate invariant; a diagnostic reveal should update it. Fail on world-truth leakage, missed reveal updates, or gains that vanish when names and layouts change.

3. **P2 — Experiment with a small joint belief–preference likelihood model.** Only after P0/P1, introduce an offline predictor at OtherModel's hypothesisDistribution/sealPredictions boundary. Enumerate a bounded set of joint belief/reward hypotheses, simulate observation-conditioned policies, and normalize trajectory likelihoods over that joint set. Preserve alternative explanations rather than committing prematurely to separate best belief and best desire. Cache policies and record approximation error: seven rewards for each of three trucks already imply 343 reward triples before the belief grid. Reuse event provenance to prevent duplicate evidence updates. **Validation:** compare the full model with TrueBel, NoObs, and an action-only predictor on held-out layouts and visibility transitions, including dominated paths and ambiguous trajectories. Require lower held-out action log loss with a paired 95% interval excluding zero against each baseline, no Brier-score regression, and runtime within a predeclared budget. Also evaluate recovery of simulator-known beliefs/preferences separately from agreement with human attributions. Reject production adoption if benefits depend on the original layouts, disappear under modest perception/policy noise, or require sharply confident explanations of behavior the model cannot predict.

**Verdict: experiment.** Adopt the separation of world state, actor information, subjective belief, and observer uncertainty as a design constraint. First close the specific semantic-validation gap identified by Q21S. The paper gives a useful small-domain reference model and strong comparative evidence for observation-dependent joint attribution; it supplies neither an open-world user model nor evidence that Navi should infer durable preferences from every puzzling action. Stable reward functions and known visibility are particularly fragile assumptions in conversation, where social signaling and changing intentions also shape behavior.
