# A Definition of Open-Ended Learning for Goal-Conditioned Agents

**Authors:** Olivier Sigaud, Gianluca Baldassarre, Cédric Colas, Stéphane Doncieux, Richard Duro, Pierre-Yves Oudeyer, Nicolas Perrin-Gilbert, and Vieri Giuliano Santucci. Sigaud and Baldassarre contributed equally.

**Reviewer:** GPT-6 Astra. **Implementation reference:** [IMPLEMENTATION.md](IMPLEMENTATION.md), 2026-09-06, git HEAD `ee1590009d6fd4c48035c63e15b3896a585757d7`; section identifiers below refer to the shared master's static inspection.

**Central judgment.** Adopt the paper's separation of novelty, competence, retention, and goal provenance as an evaluation discipline. Do not interpret persistent activity or expanding memory as demonstrated open-ended learning. The definition deliberately admits processes with no competence improvement, and its claimed implication from novel goals to necessarily continuing learning is stronger than its premises support. Navi needs an independently verified account of acquired and retained capabilities before an open-endedness claim becomes useful.

**Core contribution and mechanism.** This is a conceptual taxonomy and definition paper, rather than a new learning algorithm. The historical discussion distinguishes open-ended environments that supply challenges from agents that autonomously develop skills (§2, pp. 2–7). The authors reject requiring every desirable developmental property in one definition: intrinsic motivation, autonomy, abstraction, and lifelong retention should be separately specified.

Their elementary object is a process emitting arbitrary “tokens,” which can represent phenotypes, observations, policies, reward functions, or goals. These are mathematical objects, not specifically language-model tokens. An observer regards a process as open-ended when, for every time `t`, some later `t′ > t` produces a token novel according to that observer (§3.1, Key definition 1, p. 8). Novelty concerns difference from previously emitted tokens. Repetition between discoveries is allowed; neither a minimum discovery rate nor increasing complexity is required. The paper proves that the cumulative number of distinct tokens must be unbounded by repeatedly applying this condition. The reasoning presumes a coherent cumulative judgment of distinctness; an observer that simply forgets old tokens cannot support the same interpretation.

The authors then define history-dependent RL problems through observations, actions, transitions, and rewards, without imposing a Markov state representation. A sequence of problems is open-ended if an appropriate defining component is generated by an open-ended process (§3.2, pp. 8–9). Goal-conditioned RL adds a goal space `G` and reward `R(h_t, g)`; an open-ended goal generator makes the GCRL problem open-ended (§3.3, p. 9). This defines the challenge stream separately from the solver.

First-order open-endedness generates goals within one space; second-order open-endedness generates an open-ended sequence of goal representation spaces and goals within them (§3.4, p. 10). Neither guarantees interesting development. The authors explicitly admit trivial examples: counting to ever-larger integers, targeting ever-closer real-valued speeds, and trivial diversity between goal spaces. A more demanding observer can reject these as insufficiently novel, but the paper does not provide that observer.

The remaining distinctions are useful: behavioral goals condition action, while achieved goals describe actual outcomes (§4.1, p. 11); lifelong learning additionally requires retaining prior goal-solving ability (§4.2, p. 12). Goals may be extrinsic, internally generated/autotelic, or influenced by social partners/teachable (§4.3, pp. 12–13). Receiving useful guidance therefore need not disqualify an agent from developmental learning. These categories describe origins and capabilities; they are not implemented curricula supplied by this paper.

**Evidence, limitations, and disagreements.** The source offers definitions, an elementary unboundedness argument, illustrative counterexamples, and a qualitative literature synthesis. It contains no new agent experiment, measured improvement, benchmark comparison, or quantitative developmental trajectory. Figure 1 is historical and Figure 2 organizes definitions. Claims about effective frontier curricula refer to earlier research (§4.3); this review has not independently evaluated those cited papers.

The most consequential weakness is in §4.1, p. 11. Definition Sol3 requires an RL agent to *solve* an open-ended problem, whereas Sol4 only requires a GCRL agent to *use* goals from an open-ended generator. Neither the latter wording nor goal novelty ensures learning or success. Nevertheless, the following paragraph claims optimal agents must continually learn to solve the novel goals.

A counterexample follows from the paper's own counting illustration. Let goals be successive integers `N`, with reward for stopping at count `N`. A fixed policy, “increment while the observed count is below N, then stop,” solves every goal without further acquisition. A numerical-identity observer accepts the goal sequence as open-ended. Thus infinitely many novel goals can be handled by one already acquired general rule, even with only increment/stop primitive actions. Continuing learning would require additional restrictions on the agent class or challenges that exceed existing competence. The paper's broad history-conditioned policy representation does not supply those restrictions. This criticizes its asserted implication, without adjudicating the separate continual-RL paper it discusses.

Second-order status also depends on representation conventions. Multiple goal spaces can be encoded as one tagged union, and renaming spaces can simulate expansion. This is an analyst inference from §3.4: claiming representational development needs an operational criterion for a newly expressible outcome or distinction, beyond a new schema name. Conversely, a fixed primitive action vocabulary does not rule out rich new compositions or goals.

Evaluation is necessarily finite (§5, pp. 13–14). The proposed intrinsic-learning phase followed by representative held-out tasks measures useful acquisition, which the authors correctly distinguish from proving open-endedness. Their alternative extrapolates cumulative novel-goal curves: saturation suggests failure, whereas logarithmic or faster unbounded growth qualifies asymptotically. A finite observed curve cannot establish either asymptote. Identical observed prefixes can precede permanent saturation or arbitrarily delayed discoveries. Moreover, representing the “whole” task space is underspecified when new goal spaces can emerge. Report finite-budget coverage and transfer under an explicit sampling distribution, rather than an infinity certificate.

Section 6, p. 14 explicitly concedes that the definition implies no performance progress and leaves competence, discovery, abstraction, and creativity measures unresolved. This candor makes the paper valuable for measurement design, while limiting its authority as an architectural prescription.

The defensible current characterization is a persistent executive with several genuine online learning mechanisms and externally supplied goal support. Whether it develops useful new capabilities over time remains unverified. Functional goal conditioning is an architectural analogy here, not evidence that the complete executive optimizes the paper's GCRL reward objective or establishes consciousness.

**Prioritized recommendations.** These are proposed documentation and isolated evaluation work, not experiments performed or production changes made by this review.

1. **P0 — Specify the observer and separate discovery from mastery.** Create an offline evaluation ledger derived from intention, ActionTrace, ActionExecution, and ProcedureRun identities (I05; Q29, Q36–Q37). Each evaluated goal should carry provenance, a versioned outcome predicate, goal-family/representation identity, first exposure, attempts, and independently verified results. Report distinct proposed, attempted, first-achieved, and repeatably mastered goals separately. Count numerical instances separately from qualitatively new goal families. This implements the paper's observer and behavioral/achieved distinction without making every new title a discovery. Dependencies are domain-specific outcome verifiers and explicit novelty adjudication; semantic annotation and historical comparison have real costs. **Validation/failure:** use a prespecified challenge set containing paraphrases, ID changes, cycles longer than 24 thoughts, easy parameter variations, and substantively different outcomes. A second annotator checks novelty decisions. If equivalent goals inflate family counts, or failed/unverified tasks enter mastery counts, reject the metric before using its growth curve.

2. **P1 — Measure acquisition and retention at matched budgets.** Adapt §5's two-phase evaluation using isolated, identical starting states and a declared task distribution. Measure initial competence, then allow a bounded experience phase, then evaluate new instances and previously mastered tasks. Hold the foundation model, prompts, task exposure, and inference budgets comparable. Contrast normal updates with disabled persistence of newly learned memory/procedures; separately ablate procedural reuse to localize benefit. Relevant boundaries are token-memory publication/retrieval (I02), `ExecutiveCore::integrateConsolidation:5020`, and `ProceduralMemory::observe:399` (Q17, Q36). This is a transfer evaluation adapted to a pretrained executive; the paper's no-hardwired-knowledge intrinsic phase cannot be reproduced literally. Costs include cloned state, independent scoring, and enough repetitions for uncertainty estimates. **Validation/failure:** proposed preregistered thresholds are a 10-percentage-point held-out success gain over the no-update control, a 95% interval excluding zero gain, and at most a 5-point old-task regression. These are engineering decision thresholds, not paper results. Failure of any criterion blocks adoption. Increasing records or accepted thoughts cannot rescue a failed result. Report success against cumulative tokens and elapsed time as well as interactions.

3. **P2 — Experiment with a curriculum only after those measures work.** As explicitly speculative synthesis of §4.3, propose goals near a measured competence frontier within existing authorized task families. An isolated sampler alongside `MotivationCompiler::rankCandidates` could compare recent versus earlier verified success on fixed probe instances, retain an exploration quota, and emit goal proposals for the existing intention boundary. It would add machinery absent in Q32/Q36, not activate a dormant RL learner. Preserve externally suggested goals as a separately attributed stream so teachability can be compared under the same teaching policy (§5.2). Costs are reliable comparable trials, sparse-data uncertainty, and additional evaluation calls; with four installed adapters (I05), the available task ecology may limit benefits. **Validation/failure:** compare against uniform sampling and a fixed externally supplied curriculum at equal budget. Reject adoption if it fails to improve held-out competence/retention, or achieves apparent progress only by selecting easier tasks or relabeling goals. Defer dynamic goal-space expansion until existing representations demonstrably prevent expressing useful outcomes.

**Verdict: adopt the conceptual distinctions; experiment with finite-budget competence evaluation; defer a production curriculum.** The next consequential step is a versioned goal/achievement observer paired with acquisition and retention measurements. An endless succession of novel sentences is compatible with the paper's weakest notion of novelty and with no useful learning. The proposed evidence must distinguish those cases.
