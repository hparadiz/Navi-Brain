# Intention Is Choice with Commitment

Philip R. Cohen and Hector J. Levesque. *Artificial Intelligence* 42 (1990), 213–261. Local journal version; revised manuscript received October 1988. [Source PDF](../../../assets/papers/05-cohen-levesque-1990-intention-choice-commitment.pdf). Reviewer: **GPT-6 Astra**. Implementation evidence: [shared implementation reference](IMPLEMENTATION.md), snapshot **2026-09-06**, revision `ee1590009d6fd4c48035c63e15b3896a585757d7`; supplied by the implementation master, without independent code inspection.

**Central finding.** Navi-Brain already has durable intentions and action evidence, but automatic completion stores `released` while emitting `completed`, and the dependency compiler treats both as ready (§Q05). This paper supplies the missing semantic distinction: achievement and justified abandonment are different exits from commitment. Adopt that distinction as an executive specification. The paper does not establish that persistence alone produces effective planning, and implementing its full modal logic would miss its explicit architectural advice.

Page references below give **PDF page / printed page**. Coverage includes all 49 pages, §§1–10 and references; there are no appendices. Essential definitions and theorem statements were visually checked on PDF pages 24, 26, 27, 33, 36 and 42 because extraction corrupts some logical symbols. No experiments, tests or runtime actions were performed for this review.

**The mechanism is constrained abandonment.** The foundational operators are belief (`BEL`), choice-compatible goals (`GOAL`), next events (`HAPPENS`) and completed events (`DONE`). Worlds are event sequences; belief and goal accessibility vary with event index. `GOAL` describes what follows from an agent's chosen worlds, including unwanted but believed-inevitable consequences. It does not mean every such consequence is deliberately pursued. Goals are consistent, and chosen worlds must remain compatible with beliefs (`G ⊆ B`; §§3.2–3.6, PDF 12–23 / 224–235).

Definition 4.1 (PDF 24 / 236) adds persistence to an achievement goal. In compact notation:

`PG(x,p) = GOAL(x,LATER(p)) ∧ BEL(x,¬p) ∧ BEFORE(BEL(x,p) ∨ BEL(x,□¬p), ¬GOAL(x,LATER(p)))`.

Here `LATER(p) = ¬p ∧ ◇p`. The agent chooses eventual achievement, believes the target currently false, and cannot abandon that achievement goal before believing it achieved or forever unachievable. `BEFORE` permits the conditions to coincide; this is an ordering constraint, not a prescribed delay. Achievement may leave a maintenance goal behind. The paper explicitly sets maintenance goals aside (§3.6.1, PDF 21 / 233), so this lifecycle should not be imposed indiscriminately on standing values or continuing needs.

Intention adds action-specific conditions. `INTEND₁` commits to having performed an action while believing beforehand that it was about to occur; merely reaching the verge of action is insufficient (§6.1, PDF 33 / 245). `INTEND₂` allows commitment to personally achieving a state without identifying an entire event sequence in advance. It constrains the relationship between anticipated and actual action so accidental achievement need not count as fulfillment (§6.2, PDF 35–37 / 247–249). This supports distinguishing “the state now holds,” “my action achieved it,” and “I executed the intended action.” The paper acknowledges that it does not adequately formalize the causal connection between intention and behavior.

Two consequences matter operationally. Failed attempts leave intentions in force when the agent believes the target unachieved and does not believe it impossible (Theorem 7.2, PDF 39 / 251). Predicted success also leaves them in force: the Little Nell problem arises when a planner cancels a rescue because its own plan predicts Nell will be safe (§7.2, PDF 40–42 / 252–254). A forecast derived from the intention is not evidence that its target already holds.

The final account deliberately weakens “fanatical” persistence. Definition 8.1 adds `BEL(x,¬q)` as another permissible release ground, where `q` describes the commitment's background justification (§8.1, PDF 42–44 / 254–256). Background can include a parent goal or another agent's continued request. Crucially, believing the justification false differs from simply lacking belief in it. Its failure **permits** abandonment; the definition does not universally force cancellation. Parent-child dependencies therefore need explicit meanings, particularly when a child serves multiple purposes.

**The evidence is formal and assumption-dependent.** The paper offers definitions, propositions, proofs and philosophical counterexamples, not a benchmark comparison. Its reference to a goal/belief dependency network in SRI's Flakey illustrates an architectural realization (§9, PDF 45–46 / 257–258); it supplies no measured effect size, success rate or cost comparison.

The strongest eventual-achievement claim, Theorem 4.5 (PDF 27 / 239), depends on three substantial premises: the goal is persistent; the agent is always correct whenever it believes the target achieved; and it does not conclude impossibility before abandoning the goal. Assumption 3.25 additionally requires eventual abandonment of every achievement goal (PDF 21 / 233). Persistence restricts the exit conditions, and eventual abandonment forces one to occur. This is a specification of admissible histories, not an algorithm that discovers a plan, learns impossibility, or guarantees fair scheduling. There is no deadline bound. A bounded worker queue and a model confidence threshold do not establish those premises.

Other idealizations materially limit transfer. Beliefs have logical closure and positive/negative introspection; beliefs and goals are individually consistent. Primitive actions are known retrospectively, and agents settle which primitive event they believe happens next. The world model excludes simultaneous primitive actions and lacks a causal primitive (§§3, 5, 10). Navi's asynchronous processes require concrete stale-result and dispatch rules beyond this semantics.

The side-effect result is also narrower than “intentions exclude all collateral consequences.” Persistence need not transfer through a revisable belief linking the intended outcome to a side effect. But the authors admit that stronger, permanently believed or logical implications can force unwanted consequences into persistent goals in some cases (§4.1.2, Table 1, PDF 25–26 / 237–238). Their own proposed remedy is finer semantics. An engineering representation should explicitly distinguish target outcomes, instrumental subgoals, predictions and constraints; labeling an effect unintended does not make it acceptable.

The bounded implementation questions were answered in §Q05 before finalization. `closeIntention:1471` requires a valid status and note but checks neither achievement evidence nor release-condition truth, and does not compare caller authority with intention authority. This is a function-boundary finding, not a claim that an external operator lacked permission. Automatic completion prompts explicitly reject effort/proximity as success, but their integrator requires no evidence references. Its write at `:9896` stores `released` while its event and return value say `completed`; direct completed closure stores `completed`. Consumers therefore cannot safely infer a single outcome semantics from those labels.

Existing guards must be preserved: `startAction` checks its own intention is active. Separately, `evaluateSelfPresenceThread:8938`, `evaluateEpistemicAdvanceThread:9075`, `evaluateMindStreamThread:9262` and `selfPresenceSpeechGate:11420` enforce active user-authority parents for particular thread/speech paths. These checks concern `CognitiveThread.parent_intention_id`, not general intention dependencies. Already-started action dispatch has narrower action/procedure checks, without an established atomic intention-revocation invariant (§Q05). Live behavior remains unverified.

**Priority 1 — Align closure semantics and enforce evidence-bearing transitions.** First make canonical status, event and returned outcome agree in `ExecutiveCore::integrateCompletionCheck`, and distinguish fulfillment from release throughout `closeIntention` and `IntentionCompiler::dependencyStates` (§Q05). Define whether each dependency requires achievement or merely termination; release must not satisfy an achievement prerequisite by default. Historical `released` records may be ambiguous, so preserve provenance rather than blindly relabeling them. Then consolidate closure checks and record a typed transition reason, target predicate/version, supporting evidence IDs, and whether success requires personal execution. Retain prose for targets that cannot yet be mechanically evaluated, but label the semantic judgment and its provenance. Separate predicted achievement, observed achievement, temporary blockage and evidenced impossibility.

This follows directly from §§4, 6 and the Little Nell argument. Dependencies are a limited predicate vocabulary and trustworthy outcome attribution; arbitrary natural-language success remains expensive and fallible. Do not require that every target be achieved by Navi: externally achieved state goals may close legitimately, while action-specific goals require different evidence.

Proposed evaluation: replay independently labeled scenarios in a future isolated evaluation environment, comparing current closure decisions with the proposed boundary. Include a plan predicting success before execution, a successful adapter with an unmet end condition, accidental/external achievement, a failed attempt with an alternative, verified fulfillment, and release without fulfillment. Failure is disagreement among stored/event/returned outcomes, a released prerequisite enabling an achievement-dependent child, premature completion, or rejection of valid completion. Ablate evidence type and actor attribution separately. These are proposed criteria, not results.

**Priority 2 — Make justification changes actionable at dispatch.** Build on `IntentionCompiler` dependencies and `DecisionStateMachine::integrate`, with enforcement at the actual action-dispatch boundary (§§I05, Q02, Q23). Represent supporting conditions as typed, versioned references with explicit meanings: prerequisite completed, parent still pursuing a goal, request still authorized, or bounded budget still available. Evaluate them as supported/refuted/unknown. Unknown evidence should trigger appropriate suspension or clarification, not a fabricated belief that the goal is impossible.

When a relevant condition changes, schedule reconsideration and prevent queued proposals from committing against a revoked authorization or obsolete dependency version. Distinguish a domain reason permitting release from explicit withdrawal that the system's authority policy requires it to obey. The latter enforcement is an engineering addition; §8's logical permission to drop a goal is insufficient by itself. Parent completion may enable a prerequisite while invalidating a different child's sole purpose. Avoid unconditional descendant cancellation.

Dependencies and costs include version checks, reverse dependency indexing, bounded traversal of cycles and multiple supports, and handling actions already in flight. Specify the irreversible dispatch boundary; no mechanism can undo an external effect already committed. Evaluation should vary the ordering of proposal creation, context change, integration and dispatch. Failure is any dispatch crossing that boundary after authorization revocation, cancellation of a still-supported child, or abandonment solely because supporting evidence became unavailable. Measure reconsideration latency and unnecessary re-proposals, and ablate the final version check. Budget exhaustion should produce an explicit context-based release or blocked state, never invented success or metaphysical impossibility.

**Priority 3 — Experiment with a small admissibility screen.** At `createIntention` and `ProceduralMemory::inspectCandidate:138`, add checks only for declared conflicts involving current commitments: consuming an exclusive resource, violating a required ordering, or invalidating another target before its deadline. Theorem 7.1's screen of admissibility (PDF 38 / 250) explains why merely refusing logically contradictory goal strings is insufficient: a proposed action can destroy another goal's attainability.

Start with the four installed adapters and explicitly modeled effects (§I05); this paper does not justify expanding action authority or installing a general theorem prover. Where an adapter's effects cannot be characterized reliably, label the decision unknown instead of claiming conflict freedom. Keep collateral predictions distinct from achievement targets so their absence does not induce repair attempts. Costs are effect annotations, stale-assumption handling and false vetoes; completeness over open-world goals is unattainable here.

Proposed evaluation: compare admission and dispatch with this screen enabled/disabled on declared resource, ordering and deadline conflicts, plus feasible controls. Failure is an accepted known conflict, a plan that deliberately makes its goal unachievable to manufacture a release condition, or systematic rejection of feasible controls. Include a side-effect-removal scenario: eliminating an unwanted collateral effect should not create a new obligation to restore it. These are bounded behavioral checks, not proof of rationality outside the modeled vocabulary.

**Verdict: adopt the lifecycle specification; experiment with bounded enforcement.** The most consequential next step is an auditable distinction between evidence of achievement and reasons permitting release, enforced wherever commitments close and queued actions dispatch. Preserve Navi's existing durable records, compilers and action traces. Reject any claim that these structures already instantiate the paper's full theory, or that its eventualities theorem proves operational competence. Its value is precise commitment semantics for finite projects, with explicit limits on how much persistence and obedience the architecture actually guarantees.
