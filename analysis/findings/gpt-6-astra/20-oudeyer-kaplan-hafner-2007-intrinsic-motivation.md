**Central judgment.** Experiment with learning progress as a bounded exploration signal. The paper's strongest contribution is the requirement to compare improvement within similar activities: surprise alone rewards noise, while a global error decrease can reward merely switching to easier work. Navi has real prediction, learning, and motivation machinery, but the master's targeted search finds no corresponding progress estimator driving exploration. Its novelty need can instead be satisfied by constructing or reusing an unverified synthetic artifact. That is the most consequential implementation mismatch.

**Mechanism and assumptions.** The paper distinguishes error maximization, global progress maximization, and similarity-based progress maximization (§III, pp. 3–5). Its Intelligent Adaptive Curiosity (IAC) belongs to the third group. A robot predicts the next sensory vector from current sensation plus a candidate motor vector. Experiences partition this continuous sensorimotor input space into regions, each with a local predictor and its own prediction-error history (§IV, pp. 5–8).

Regions are learned from predictive structure. A region splits when its exemplar count exceeds a threshold, illustrated with 250. An axis-aligned input cut minimizes the child-size-weighted variance of **next sensory outputs**, not the variance of learning progress. Children receive their corresponding exemplars and local experts; the experiments use nearest-neighbor prediction. Both children also inherit the parent's error history (§IV-C–E, pp. 6–7).

Using a region-local visit index \(k\), the paper's equations can be rewritten:

\[
e_r(k)=\lVert S_k-\widehat S_k\rVert^2,\qquad
\bar e_r(k)=\frac{1}{\theta+1}\sum_{i=0}^{\theta}e_r(k-i),\qquad
LP_r(k)=\bar e_r(k-\tau)-\bar e_r(k).
\]

The typical parameters are \(\tau=15\), \(\theta=25\): two **overlapping 26-observation averages**, separated by 15 local visits. Time here advances when that expert receives an example, not on every robot action. Equations (1)–(2) define **signed** improvement; worsening is negative, rather than rewarded through an absolute difference. These details matter when translating the mechanism into asynchronous cognitive work (§IV-E, p. 7).

For selection, each candidate action receives the latest progress estimate of its covering region. IAC exploits the highest estimate while retaining random exploration. Although §IV-F presents a general reinforcement-learning interpretation and a Q-learning update, the experiments deliberately optimize only the next intrinsic reward, using the recent regional slope as its estimate, equation (3). They do not demonstrate long-horizon reinforcement learning (§IV-F, pp. 7–8).

The method assumes that regional histories remain comparable, recent slopes predict near-future improvement, candidate actions can reach useful regions, and the learning machinery can improve there. A “progress niche” depends jointly on environment, embodiment, predictor, and learning history; it is not an objective property of a topic (§VIII-B1, p. 18). Intermediate difficulty is an emergent tendency under those conditions, not a theorem guaranteeing a particular difficulty level.

**Evidence and its limits.** The first experiment uses a Webots robot with wheel-speed and sound-frequency actions. Different sound ranges make a toy move randomly, remain stationary, or jump toward the robot, creating deliberately distinct unlearnable, harder learnable, and easy regimes. IAC's trajectory shifts from easy prediction toward the more demanding wheel-dependent relation (§VI, pp. 9–12; Figs. 4–5).

The useful controlled comparison keeps the predictive architecture while changing selection to IAC, maximum predicted error (MAX), or RANDOM. Every 100 actions, the learner is frozen and evaluated on an independently collected dataset restricted to the nontrivial learnable regime. RANDOM initially learns this regime faster; IAC overtakes after reallocating attention. Across 100 repetitions, reported mean plateau times are **4,583 actions, SD 452**, for IAC and **11,980, SD 561**, for RANDOM: approximately 2.6 times faster (§VI-E, pp. 11–12; Fig. 6). This supports sample efficiency on the chosen learnable subproblem, not dominance at every horizon or over every active-learning method.

The physical AIBO experiment supplies stronger embodiment evidence but substantial scaffolding: three prebuilt motor primitives, five motor parameters, three engineered binary sensors, tagged objects, and action intervals chosen to let objects return to rest. This deliberately makes the environment effectively memoryless between trials (§VII-A–C, pp. 12–13). Observed trajectories progress through looking, poorly directed biting/bashing, and affordance-sensitive interactions (Fig. 8, p. 14).

Crucially, the **200-run statistics are simulator results**. The authors first collect several thousand physical interactions, fit a locally weighted regression environment model, then run IAC against that model. Table I reports the complete developmental scenario in 34% of runs, the less constrained scenario in 53%, and biting before bashing in 92% (§VII-D, pp. 15–16). These establish variability and ordering in the fitted environment. The paper does not quantify held-out surrogate fidelity, specify an exact physical-run count, or provide a matched multi-policy physical evaluation. Nor does it supply a chance-distribution comparison establishing that 34% is unexpectedly high.

Visual verification exposed local reporting inconsistencies. The repeated-run MSE paragraph prints **0.89/0.96**, whereas nearby text and Fig. 6 describe values around **0.09/0.096** (pp. 11–12). Table I's successful-bash row prints **78% yes and 11% no** (p. 15). Also, §IV-F calls exploration probability 0.35 typical, while §VI-E cites 0.15 for its illustrated run. The first two are unresolved numerical inconsistencies; the last leaves experiment-specific parameterization incompletely explained. I retain the clear sample-count result without silently repairing error magnitudes or percentages.

**Interpretation and disagreement.** This is convincing evidence for self-organized allocation within a designed learning space. It is weaker evidence for open-ended cumulative development: the robot discovers useful settings of supplied primitives, and the experiments do not isolate whether learning an earlier activity causally enables a later skill. Hierarchical skills, transfer across representations, emergent goals, and delayed access to progress niches remain explicit research problems (§VIII-A, pp. 16–18; §IX, p. 20). The infant-development discussion is conceptual interpretation, expressly not a validated model of human development (§VIII-B, pp. 18–19), and supplies no evidence about consciousness.

Regional smoothing reduces the confounding from activity switching but cannot eliminate changes in example difficulty **within** a region, fluctuating noise, or predictor resets. Copying a parent's mixed history into both children creates a possible transient attribution problem. Output-variance splitting need not discover the partition that best separates learning rates. These are deductions from the algorithm, not failures measured by the paper. Likewise, an error rise can reflect forgetting or environmental change; signed progress alone is not a recovery policy. Comparing progress across different representations is explicitly unresolved (§IX, p. 20): sensor MSE, categorical forecast loss, and factual-task error are not automatically commensurate.

I also disagree with treating predefined-task evaluation as generally unsuitable (§V, p. 8). Developmental trajectory measures are useful, but Navi needs independent task competence and resource-cost measurements alongside them. Interesting activity sequences alone cannot justify additional compute.

**Mapping to Navi-Brain.** The relevant transfer is an executive measurement and allocation mechanism, with real learning substrates beneath it.

**Prioritized recommendations — proposed evaluations, not performed experiments.**

1. **P1: Establish a trustworthy progress signal before rewarding it.** Add a bounded observer beside `ForwardModel::observe` and the learning/outcome integration paths, recording prediction-before-outcome, target, source/task family, learner or memory version, and regional visit count. Start with fixed comparable families, one target type and loss scale, and the signed IAC estimator. Require sufficient observations; the current 12-error precision window cannot reproduce the paper's 41-observation span. In parallel, compare frozen pre/post learner versions on the same independent cases to separate actual learning from an easier stream. This additional control is a proposed extension to IAC. Cost is retained error history plus paired evaluations; begin with cheap predictors. **Failure criterion:** apparent positive progress persists in a frozen learner when only task mix changes, or fails to predict held-out improvement. Include learnable, mastered, irreducibly noisy, and drifting regimes. Do not use model confidence or artifact count as truth labels.

2. **P2: Pilot progress-based choice within a bounded, authorized exploration budget.** After P1 succeeds, supply per-family progress estimates to an isolated trial of `DecisionStateMachine::integrate`, after installed-adapter validation. Keep external commitments and feasibility constraints outside the curiosity reward. Compare equal-action and equal-compute budgets against existing scoring, RANDOM, MAX-error, and global unpartitioned progress. Retain bounded exploration so stale regions can be revisited. Progress per unit cost is a useful **proposed extension**, since the paper chiefly counts actions. Dependencies include enough eligible candidates, attributable outcomes, and cost instrumentation; changing `ActionSelector` alone would not establish dispatch. Predeclare held-out error at fixed compute as the primary outcome. **Failure criterion:** paired repeated-run 95% confidence intervals do not establish improvement over RANDOM and existing scoring, stationary noise is repeatedly selected beyond the exploration allowance, or committed work is starved. Add a delayed-payoff case to expose the immediate-reward limitation. Do not automatically convert daydream production into epistemic credit.

3. **P3: Earn adaptive regions and sustained-development claims through ablation.** Only after fixed families work, compare them with bounded output-variance splits modeled on §IV-C. Cap retained exemplars, region count, and tree depth; a per-leaf example limit does not bound lifetime memory. Track split lineage and compare inherited versus re-estimated child histories to detect artificial reward spikes. Evaluate mixtures where a single family contains both noisy and learnable subregions, plus later return to previously mastered tasks. **Failure criterion:** splits increase apparent progress without held-out benefit, or previously learned performance degrades while novelty metrics improve. Cost includes partition maintenance and retention evaluation. Use identically initialized disposable stores and a verified neutral query path where available; otherwise account explicitly for query learning within each condition. **QMEM** verifies that candidate-read-neutral ranking can still reinforce token links, so an evaluation read must not silently become training.

**Verdict: experiment, with measurement first.** Adopt the distinction between surprise, current predictability, and comparable-context learning progress now. Prototype the latter independently before changing need satisfaction or production scheduling. The valuable promise is a more selective use of learning opportunities; autonomous general development remains an unproven extrapolation.
