EMA's most useful contribution to Navi-Brain is a separation of responsibilities: heterogeneous perception and reasoning processes maintain an explicit interpretation of what events mean for particular concerns; a comparatively cheap appraisal function evaluates that evolving interpretation; coping selects operations that change it or the world. Navi already has executable affect and behavioral modulation. The consequential transfer is to ground these effects in identifiable outcomes, remedies, and subsequent evidence. **Experiment with that transfer; do not treat this paper as validation of a complete human emotion model or as a prescription to reproduce every coping strategy.**

The central theoretical claim is that emotional dynamics need not require separate fast and slow appraisal systems. Appraisal itself can remain shallow, automatic, and parallel while the representations it reads change through fast perception, slower inference, and action. Apparent sequencing of appraisals then reflects information availability rather than an invariant order of appraisal checks (PDF pp. 2–7, §§1.3–2.2). This is a claim about the location of computation within an architecture. It does not eliminate expensive reasoning or demonstrate that all relevant inferences are cheap.

EMA makes that distinction concrete with a blackboard-like **causal interpretation**: propositions, past actions, future plans, preconditions and effects, causal establishment and threat links, beliefs, intentions, probabilities, and utilities. Explicit intention distinguishes a contemplated action from a commitment to act. Cognitive operators add or retract beliefs, plans, and intentions, orient perception, or initiate and terminate actions. Built on SOAR, EMA adopts sequential cognitive operators and fast parallel reactive processing; the cited 50 ms operator step is an architectural assumption (PDF pp. 8–10, §§2.3.1–2.3.2, Fig. 3, Table 1).

An appraisal frame attaches to each represented proposition. Relevance follows intrinsic or causally derived utility; desirability evaluates benefit or harm from a perspective; likelihood concerns occurrence; controllability asks whether the appraising agent has an action that can alter the outcome; changeability asks about another causal agent. Causal attribution concerns responsibility, potentially including intention and foreknowledge. These distinctions prevent probability, importance, and ability to intervene from collapsing into one confidence number (PDF p. 11, §2.3.3).

Emotion labels summarize appraisal patterns but do not constitute the coping algorithm. Table 2 distinguishes hope/joy and fear/sadness by uncertain versus certain desirable or undesirable outcomes. Anger includes negative desirability, attribution to another, and controllability **not low**; the PDF's inequality is easily lost in extraction. Labels can overlap. Each emotion's frame intensities also aggregate through a sigmoid into mood, which biases selection among frames activated by the latest cognitive operator. The selected frame's appraisal configuration determines coping. Thus “single-level appraisal” coexists with separate appraisal-frame and aggregate-mood representations; it does not mean one scalar affect state (PDF pp. 12–13, §2.3.4, Table 2 and footnote 7).

Coping supplies the reverse path. Strategies identify and modify the representational features responsible for an appraisal, enabling or suppressing cognitive operators. Attention strategies monitor or suppress information; belief strategies include responsibility shifting and wishful thinking; desire strategies change utility; intention strategies include planning, support seeking, procrastination, resignation, avoidance, and making amends. Proposals compete and are adopted sequentially, with preferences conditioned on control and changeability. Subsequent observation or inference changes the interpretation and triggers reappraisal (PDF pp. 13–14, §2.3.5). This is a specific control loop, substantially richer than appending an emotional adjective to a prompt.

The paper's new evidence is principally a **worked naturalistic illustration**. A pigeon interrupted a videotaped study involving two actors. The authors interpret one actor's expressions and movements as surprise, fear, defensive aggression, relaxation, concern for the bird, and helping across approximately 2.6 seconds. The video was recorded at 30 frames/s; descriptions of 30–60 ms expression changes therefore concern only roughly one or two frames, not finely resolved physiological measurements (PDF pp. 2–3, §1.1, Fig. 1).

The simulation supplies an authored domain of eight propositions and seven actions. A sound prompts orientation; perception of an approaching bird prompts an inferred attack; poor immediate defense options favor avoidance; moving away makes umbrella use feasible; increased control changes the appraisal and favors planning; the bird becoming caught removes the threat and makes helping relevant (PDF pp. 15–18, §3, Figs. 4–7). This demonstrates that one appraisal mechanism can generate a plausible sequence as its input changes.

It does **not** establish that the actor used those representations or that EMA outperforms competing process theories. The authors explicitly allow alternative encodings. Bird-intention recognition is simulated with a domain-specific rule because the corresponding cognitive operator is missing (PDF p. 16, footnote 10). Orientation is scripted to reveal the bird about 200 ms later based on video analysis; physical effects have authored delays. The discussion calls these free parameters and the timing model underconstrained (PDF pp. 15, 19). There is no new controlled comparison, held-out prediction score, effect size, or timing-error distribution here. Earlier human-vignette and social-causality evaluations are cited, but their underlying results are not reproduced sufficiently to evaluate independently from this source.

The illustration also exposes a substantive failure: when the injured bird becomes helpable, EMA predicts **anger**, whereas the observed response appears closer to empathy or “fear for.” Helping behavior can therefore coexist with an implausible emotion label. The proposed extension to other-directed appraisals is a suggested repair, not a validated result (PDF pp. 17–19, §4). Other limitations are architectural: current/past propositions are assumed fully observable; implemented unexpectedness detects unexplained changes rather than disconfirmed explicit probabilistic expectations; quantitative intensity details are deferred to earlier work; bodily dynamics are represented only indirectly through mood (PDF pp. 10–12, footnotes 3–4; p. 15). These restrictions matter for an executive with incomplete sensors and asynchronous evidence.

Three priorities follow. They are proposed evaluations and changes, not experiments performed for this review.

1. **P1 — Prototype a concern-specific appraisal projection before changing behavior.** Extend the semantics around `Appraisal`/`appraise`, alongside `appraiseNow`, with a bounded projection containing concern ID, predicted outcome, affected goal/agent, source evidence and revision, separate likelihood and signed desirability, and known authorized remedies. Derive control from remedy feasibility, preserving “unknown” when no assessment exists. Reuse existing event/intention links, `IntentionCompiler`, working-memory provenance, action traces, and adapter inspection (I03, I05, Q08); keep worker health as its own input. This is an engineering adaptation of §§2.3.1–2.3.3, not a claim that current records already provide calibrated utility. Cost lies chiefly in defining outcome/goal semantics and retaining unresolved alternatives. Evaluate paired traces with identical worker status counts but different goal consequences, and with identical threats but different available remedies. Pass only if the affected frame changes in the intended dimension with cited evidence; unrelated concern values must remain unchanged before mood aggregation. Failure to distinguish these cases, or inventing control from model availability alone, rejects the projection.

2. **P2 — Add a small, attributable coping experiment.** Once P1 is credible, connect selected frames to a bounded proposal for targeted observation, reconsideration of an existing plan, or deferral until a specified external condition. Candidate validation belongs at the existing decision/procedural boundaries; any within-cycle retrieval or re-proposal loop would be a new mechanism because Q03 verifies only initial retrieval and later-cycle reconsideration. Log the originating appraisal revision, selected operation, observed result, and subsequent appraisal delta. Preserve uncertainty and action authority. Dependencies include P1 and an explicit condition for finishing a wait. On scripted traces, withdraw a threat while inference is pending, reveal a remedy, or resolve a forecast. Require the next accepted appraisal to reflect the newest evidence and the proposed response to fit the same concern. Compare against identical frames without coping feedback. Failure means stale defensive behavior, repeated observation without new evidence, or no reduction in independently labeled inappropriate actions at a fixed work budget. Successful command execution alone is insufficient.

3. **P3 — Evaluate the dynamics without fitting them to the answer.** Instrument observation receipt, interpretation revision, appraisal, proposal, dispatch, and outcome separately around `appraiseNow`, decision integration, and action completion. Q02 warns that current model timing includes queue/integration delay and that recorded totals can omit asynchronous waits; it also reports no general workspace-checksum freshness check at decision integration. Independently vary world-event, inference, and action-effect delays in a future offline replay. Compare global gauges, concern frames without coping, and the complete candidate loop; separately ablate mood bias. Predeclare acceptable response order and stale-action rate before inspecting held-out trajectories. Fail if the system needs scenario-specific appraisal delays or hand-edited emotion transitions, or if improvements disappear when controlling for information access and compute. A direct controller using the same concern variables is an essential comparison: natural-language emotion labels need not add control value, and EMA itself treats them largely as conveniences.

Adoption also requires a deliberate disagreement with some descriptive psychology. Wishful thinking changes probabilities to feel better; distancing reduces goal utility; resignation drops commitments; information suppression can hide an uncontrollable threat. These may model human coping, but they are poor default remedies for an evidence-accountable collaborator. In particular, Q05 reports that direct `closeIntention:1471` does not validate success/release evidence or compare caller authority at that boundary. Wiring affect to that operation would create a concrete failure mode: operational frustration could discard a user commitment. Represent alternative interpretations with evidence, and make commitment changes follow the existing authorization requirements rather than emotional intensity.

The broader disagreement is with treating architectural economy as empirical victory. EMA relocates complexity into domain knowledge, causal inference, and operator timing; the paper supplies neither a complexity proof nor decisive evidence against multilevel appraisal. Its strongest transferable result is a useful decomposition whose components can be instrumented and ablated. **Verdict: experiment, beginning with P1's concern projection and held-out trace criteria; retain current behavior until the new representation demonstrates useful distinctions.** This supports a functional appraisal architecture, not an inference about subjective experience. Q08 resolved the bounded implementation question: event-linked appraisal records exist, while a concern-linked causal coping loop was not found in the stated search. Runtime efficacy and calibration remain unmeasured.
