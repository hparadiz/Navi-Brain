## Core contribution

The useful contribution is a causal constraint on mental-state inference: an attributed belief should follow from what an actor could perceive and previously believed, and should help explain actions in conjunction with the actor's desires. Bayesian theory of mind (BToM) jointly infers these quantities by inverting a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). It thereby distinguishes uncertainty **inside the actor's belief** from the observer's uncertainty **about that belief** (pp. 2–3, “Computational models”).

The introductory food-truck example makes the distinction concrete. An actor bypasses visible Korean food, looks behind a building, discovers Lebanese food, then returns to Korean food. Observers infer that the actor most wanted Mexican food, which is absent, and initially believed it was behind the building. Endpoint recognition alone would identify the ultimately selected Korean truck and miss the preferred but unavailable alternative (p. 2, Fig. 1).

Equation (1), p. 3, factorizes the observer's posterior into action likelihood, actor belief updating, percept likelihood and a prior:

\[
P(B,D,P,S\mid A)\propto
P(A\mid B_1,D)P(B_1\mid P,B_0)P(P\mid S)P(B_0,D,S).
\]

The actor maintains a distribution over possible worlds, updates it from percepts, and approximately maximizes expected utility. Action probabilities follow a softmax of expected utility, with fitted rationality parameter β. Utility combines food preference and a constant movement cost. The observer maintains a joint hypothesis space of initial beliefs and utilities, with approximately uniform discrete-grid priors. Preferences remain fixed within an episode; beliefs can change. Visibility is modeled through a 360-degree isovist constrained by walls (p. 3).

This joint representation matters because preference and belief explanations can substitute for one another. Marginally plausible beliefs and desires need not constitute a plausible pair. Experiment 1 infers initial beliefs retrospectively using later trajectory evidence: this is smoothing, not a demonstration of equally accurate online inference before those actions occur (pp. 4, 9, equation (2)). Experiment 2 additionally treats the observer's world state as unknown and infers hidden cart locations through the actor's behavior (pp. 6–7).

The authors explicitly claim computational-level adequacy, not that human brains enumerate the whole hypothesis space. They use grid-based approximate planning in Experiment 1 and a point-based solver in Experiment 2, discuss alternating belief/desire inference, and acknowledge general POMDP intractability (p. 7, “Discussion”). The observer's model is first-order and nonrecursive; arbitrary nested social beliefs are future work (pp. 3, 8).

## Evidence and limitations

| Experiment | Design and sample | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| 1: beliefs and desires | 16 analyzed MIT participants; one of 17 excluded for misunderstanding. Animated trajectories; 73 analyzed scenarios from 78 valid scenarios, with five excluded as inconsistent with the rational-agency assumption. | Across individual scenarios, BToM bootstrap cross-validation correlation was **0.91** for desire ratings, 95% CI [0.89, 0.92], and **0.78** for belief ratings, [0.72, 0.85] (pp. 4–5; methods p. 9). |
| 2: hidden worlds/percepts | 176 of 200 Mechanical Turk participants; 24 excluded for server errors. Static path images; 19 scenarios, including three introductory scenarios; six location configurations rated per scenario. Known preference ordering; 24 possible worlds include cart availability. | BToM correlation was **0.91**, [0.86, 0.94], versus TrueBelief **0.63**, NoCost **0.46**, and MotionHeuristic **0.61** (pp. 6–7; methods p. 9). |

These are predictions of **mean human judgments**, not verified access to actors' actual mental states. Figure 5's 219 points are three ratings across 73 scenarios, not 219 participants. The strong result is quantitative agreement across controlled variations in geometry, visibility, incomplete trajectories and available goals, supported by useful component comparisons.

TrueBelief removes uncertainty about the world; NoCost removes the action-efficiency tradeoff. Both lose substantial predictive power. But the comparison is not a clean victory over every associative account: MotionHeuristic reaches **0.77** belief correlation in Experiment 1, essentially matching BToM's 0.78, while doing worse on desires (**0.62**) and showing compressed, clustered predictions (p. 5). Its ten fitted weights in Experiment 1 and 48 in Experiment 2 instantiate particular linear features, not the entire family of learned inference methods (p. 4). BToM's advantage concerns the combined explanatory pattern; the authors themselves consider learned feed-forward approximations to BToM (p. 8).

The statistical procedure uses 100,000 shared random splits of scenarios, fitting parameters on training scenarios and reporting median test Pearson correlations (p. 9). This controls some parameter-fitting flexibility, but does not establish calibrated probabilities or generalization to new behavioral domains. Related scenario types and repeated judgments remain structurally dependent. Holding out scenario families, actors and observation mechanisms would support stronger transfer claims. Experiment 2 fits its own β; it is not a parameter-frozen transfer demonstration.

Several boundaries materially affect adoption. Participants receive explicit task explanations; Experiment 1 displays the actor's isovist. Experiment 2 supplies preference ordering and tells participants the actor searches for the best open cart. The five rationality-based exclusions in Experiment 1 narrow the claim precisely where a deployed observer must handle surprising behavior. Adult aggregate ratings provide neither infant evidence nor process-level neural evidence. Fixed preferences, modeled perceptual access and efficient movement simplify away distraction, misunderstood interfaces, changing goals and heterogeneous effort costs (pp. 4, 8–9).

**A source-level mathematical issue requires care.** Equation (3), p. 9, visibly prints
\(P(y\mid x_{1:T})=\sum_{b_t,r}P(b_t,r\mid x_{1:T},y)P(y)\).
For a normalized conditional posterior, the sum is one, leaving the prior \(P(y)\), independent of the trajectory. This appears to be a notation/typesetting error. The intended inference requires marginalizing a *joint* posterior over world and mental state, or multiplying the world prior by its marginalized trajectory likelihood and normalizing. This observation does not show that the authors' uninspected code made the same error; it means the printed equation must not be copied literally into an implementation.

A scalar posterior strengthened by events equally predicted by incompatible propositions cannot adjudicate their content. Multi-actor generalization and production performance remain unverified; current producer/control paths primarily target `primary_user` (Q21).

## Prioritized recommendations

**P0 — Establish a content-discrimination gate before strengthening mental-state claims.** For a future offline evaluation around `OtherModelEvaluator::report` and `OtherModel::updateHypothesisFromPrediction`, define paired belief/goal hypotheses with equal priors and context but incompatible consequences. Include matched exposure histories, alternative goals and independently specified actor policies. Separate category-forecast quality from evidence for a proposition; shared speech evidence can support an activity forecast without selecting one incompatible belief. The dependency is explicit labeled fixtures and an evidence-scope contract, not more parser-generated labels. Pass only if informative observations favor the specified hypothesis and deliberately nondiagnostic observations leave relative odds unchanged. Failure includes equal likelihoods for hypotheses whose modeled action consequences differ, or confident attribution from nondiagnostic evidence. Existing coarse prediction scores remain meaningful in their own domain.

**P1 — Experiment with a small joint inverse planner and explicit actor information history.** Add an isolated prototype adjacent in responsibility to `OtherModel`, with typed world alternatives, actor observation events, episode-stable utility hypotheses and action costs. Preserve joint belief–utility hypotheses, propagate each through the actor's observation model, and score actions with a bounded softmax planner. Link eventual predictions to existing hypothesis IDs and sealing/resolution machinery. This introduces missing mechanisms identified in Q21/Q71; storage schema, observation interpretation and planning cost are the main dependencies. Initially supply structured scenes so parser failures are measured separately.

Compare the prototype against TrueBelief, NoCost, endpoint/motion cues and independent belief/desire inference on held-out scene families. Include false beliefs, unseen desired objects, incomplete paths and changes in visibility without changes in movement geometry. Require lower held-out action log loss and belief/world Brier score with paired uncertainty intervals excluding no improvement over the strongest baseline. Reject a generalization claim if gains disappear on unseen families or require trial-specific β tuning. Report wall time, hypothesis count and truncation error; broad POMDP scaling claims are not a deployment budget.

**P2 — Only after P1, examine instrumental information gathering.** The paper's window example changes what an actor can see without changing traversable routes: approaching a closer window may beat walking around the wall (p. 8). This is an untested paper prediction, not an experimental result or an explicit curiosity reward. In the prototype, compare expected downstream task utility after observation against its cost. Task-action integration would require an executable route through the decision/procedure boundary, such as `DecisionStateMachine::integrate`, whose existing scores do not supply this calculation. The inspected `ActionSelector::choose` consumer gates unprompted speech and does not dispatch a non-speech winner; changing that score alone would not implement information gathering (I04/I05, Q13S).

An offline ablation should vary observation usefulness and cost independently. A useful cheap observation should change the contingent plan; an observation unable to change the best task action should not receive positive instrumental value merely for reducing entropy. Fail adoption if the mechanism buys irrelevant information or fails to improve task reward after observation costs. Transfer from spatial visibility to interface access or delivered messages is speculative and requires its own observation model.

## Verdict

**Experiment; adopt the representational requirement now, defer production inverse planning.** The paper strongly motivates coupling actor access, beliefs, preferences and predicted actions in constrained domains. It does not establish that rational attribution remains accurate for unrestricted human behavior, nor that better social inference demonstrates consciousness. Navi-Brain already has useful records, prediction sealing and scoring; its most consequential next step is an offline demonstration that incompatible mental-state contents produce appropriately different, independently evaluated predictions. The master supplies the consequential implementation facts needed for this review; unresolved source issues are the unavailable supplement/code and the apparent error in printed equation (3).
