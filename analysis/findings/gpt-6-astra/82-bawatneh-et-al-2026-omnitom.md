**Central judgment.** Adopt the distinction between extracting a belief and evaluating its labels; experiment with the representation offline. OmniToM makes a directly relevant evaluation gap visible: Navi stores social propositions, but its activity-forecast scores cannot establish that those propositions correctly describe someone's beliefs. The paper supplies a useful diagnostic vocabulary, not an implemented persistent belief updater or evidence that explicit tables reveal a model's internal reasoning. Its noisy reference-generation/judging pipeline and an unresolved score-aggregation discrepancy limit numerical conclusions.

**Core idea and mechanism.** Instead of scoring only a final social-reasoning answer, Stage 1 maps the complete narrative to tuples `(actor, proposition, recursive order)`. The special actor `world` represents narrated facts at order zero; other rows represent first- through third-order beliefs. Stage 2 assigns seven labels: order, truth status, knowledge access, explicit/implicit representation, content type, acquisition source, and context (Sections 3–5, pp. 4–8).

Several distinctions are valuable. An attributed belief can conflict with reality; narrative support for its content is separate from the believer's confidence. Information can be private, shared within a subgroup, or public common ground. Acquisition through perception, testimony, memory, and inference is distinguished from whether a belief is explicitly stated. The ATOMS taxonomy motivates coverage, but these seven dimensions are the authors' adaptation from task-level abilities to proposition-level annotations, not a uniquely established ontology (Appendix A, pp. 13–15).

Extraction scoring uses actor-restricted semantic alignment. A judge matches rows in both directions, enforcing one-to-one bookkeeping except for compound propositions matching two or three atomic rows. Precision and recall count rows with **nonzero** MatchCount; larger counts do not directly multiply credit. Stage 2 scores exact label agreement on **supplied gold propositions**, averaging seven dimension accuracies. Consequently, 85.95% labeling accuracy is neither an end-to-end extraction-and-labeling score nor the fraction of propositions with all seven labels correct (Section 5, pp. 7–8; Appendix D.3, pp. 25–26).

Both stages see the entire story. Temporal progression is implicit in row ordering, and recursive structure remains natural-language content plus an order label. There is no executable belief transition rule, event-indexed state machine, or causal intervention on a model's internal representation.

**Evidence.** OmniToM retains seven ToMBench categories: 916 stories, of which 21—three per category—support calibration and 895 constitute the benchmark. Claude Sonnet 4.5 generates the benchmark's 22,343 propositions and 156,401 labels after achieving 72.88 extraction F1 and 93.62 labeling accuracy on calibration. The approximately 1.1K person-hours cover schema development, pilots, annotation, calibration, and adjudication; they do not mean that humans exhaustively labeled all 895 benchmark stories (Section 4, pp. 6–7; Appendix B, pp. 15–18).

The main evaluation uses zero-shot TELeR Level 3 prompts, with nine models in Stage 2 and eight in Stage 1; GPT-5 is excluded from extraction evaluation because it judges that stage. Table 3 (p. 8) reports the best extraction F1 as **57.69**, for Gemma-3 27B, and best labeling accuracy as **85.95%**, for Gemini-2.5 Flash. For Gemini, Figure 5a (p. 9) shows extraction F1 falling from **80.7** for world facts to **51.5** for first-order and **46.6** for higher-order beliefs. The pooled first-order labeling breakdown is especially weak for access (**58.9%**) and representation (**57.3%**). This supports a practical actor-attribution difficulty under this annotation protocol; it does not isolate a unique cognitive cause.

Scaffolding materially changes performance. In the 21-story prompt sweep, Llama-3.3 70B extraction rises from 45.47 at Level 3 to 67.50 at Level 4, and labeling from 82.21 to 93.48 (Table 9, p. 23). Level 4 bundles examples, formatting constraints, and extraction criteria, so this is not a clean ablation of any one ingredient. The volume audit is descriptive, and quantization where required adds another qualification to architecture/size comparisons (Section 6; Appendix D.1).

**Evidence limits and disagreements.** The most consequential limitations are methodological and representational:

- **Reference and judge uncertainty.** Human extraction overlap is 360/430 reviewed beliefs requiring no reconciliation remark, not an independent set-matching F1 or chance-corrected reliability coefficient. Three-annotator labeling agreement is 92.23% over 390 beliefs, with candidate labels pre-populated by Mistral-Small. GPT-5's judge calibration achieves only 72.03% agreement with human binary alignment decisions on 126 tables sampled from three extraction models. These are distinct quantities, neither a universal score error bar nor proof of benchmark-wide validity. The 21-story subset also selects prompts and annotator models, without a reported separate human audit validating the final 895-story annotations (Appendix B.2–B.4, pp. 16–18).
- **Aggregation needs clarification.** Section 5 specifies macro-averaging over stories, but the printed Stage 1 overall values appear to average category scores equally. An arithmetic check of Table 3 gives Gemini's seven-category mean as 54.967%, matching its reported 54.97%; weighting those category scores by Table 4's unequal story counts gives 55.750%. This calculation is a document consistency check, not a rerun. Unusable-output exclusions complicate denominators, but the paper does not reconcile the aggregation. Retain the reported numbers as reported; do not silently substitute a corrected benchmark score. Appendix D.4 (p. 26) also records substantial format losses, including Qwen3 8B's 14.41% unusable Stage 2 outputs.
- **The output representation is another behavioral target.** Figures 1–2 motivate visibility into hidden reasoning, but a generated table need not be the mechanism responsible for a separately generated answer. No experiment intervenes on the table and demonstrates that it mediates social decisions. The study does not establish that better extraction causes better interactive behavior.
- **The schema compresses distinctions needed online.** `Shared` does not enumerate the subgroup; `Temporal` does not identify the event or validity interval; exactly one context label cannot simultaneously encode an outdated belief maintained through deception. Table 14 (pp. 27–28) labels Alice's “The object is in the box” True/Temporal and Bob's identical proposition False/Temporal. The intended distinction is plausibly historical truth versus a stale current belief, but it depends on temporal interpretation absent from explicit record fields. Treat this as a warning against copying the schema as a persistent-state format, rather than declaring the example necessarily contradictory.

Order 3 supplies only 0.5% of propositions, and order 2+ partially recovers labeling accuracy because supplied nested propositions constrain labels such as Epistemic. These results do not show reliable deep recursion. Short, text-only, self-contained stories provide no measured transfer to long-running, multimodal interaction. The local version says dataset/code release is forthcoming and omits full prompt-example bodies; exact replication remains incomplete from this PDF alone (Sections 6–7; Appendices B.5–B.6 and C).

This mapping argues for separating semantic evaluation from existing predictive evaluation. It does not warrant interpreting every intentionally omitted story proposition as a current parser defect, replacing source authorization with Public/Private labels, or turning narrative truth judgments into claims of privileged access to Aku's mind.

**Prioritized recommendations.** These are proposed work, with no implementation or experiments performed in this review. Numerical thresholds below are suggested acceptance criteria, not findings from the paper.

1. **P0 — Establish an independently adjudicated semantic audit.** Extend the reporting contract around `OtherModelEvaluator::report` and its null proposition metrics using a separate offline dataset and runner, only in later authorized work. Maintain two tracks: direct-utterance cases matching the current parser's one-proposition contract, and a distinct full-story extractor experiment. Score actor/proposition precision and recall, each applicable label, joint correctness, and unusable-output rate separately. Include negation, quoted third-party beliefs, unsupported inference, and actor swaps. Freeze human reference annotations independently of candidate models; publish both story- and category-macro aggregation and failure denominators. Cost is principally human adjudication. First validate the evaluator with paraphrases, duplicate rows, wrong-actor copies, and polarity reversals: reject it if any wrong-actor or reversed-polarity control receives semantic equivalence credit. Judge disagreement must be reported, not hidden inside one F1.

2. **P1 — Prototype explicit exposure and time semantics before expanding social state.** In an isolated successor to `OtherModel::recordHypothesis` and the frame-fact representation, separate actor identity, proposition, belief-at time, content-valid time, acquisition event, and actor exposure evidence. Preserve Navi's source-authorization metadata independently; represent shared access with explicit actor sets. This is engineering synthesis motivated by OmniToM, not its implemented algorithm. Start with first-order beliefs and a bounded second-order subset, using paired observed/hidden-transfer and testimony scenarios. Compare against the same worker with textual summaries at matched context budgets, and ablate exposure links. Proposed success requires at least a ten-percentage-point gain on paired belief-state accuracy with a paired 95% confidence interval excluding zero, plus no increased unsupported attribution. Reject the added structure if gains disappear under actor renaming or event paraphrase. Dependencies include P0 annotations and explicit storage/latency budgets; the three-hypothesis live cap is not automatically the right experimental capacity.

3. **P2 — Require downstream causal benefit before production adoption.** For a successful P1 prototype, use the existing social-context publication seams (`OtherModel::publishState:692`, `decisionState:1370`; Q79/I09) in offline replay to compare correct, withheld, and actor-swapped belief structures while holding story, worker, and output budget fixed. Score independently labeled decisions about what information to explain or clarify, alongside unnecessary clarification and confident false attribution. Also compare Level 3 versus Level 4 extraction prompts to distinguish a storage benefit from added scaffolding. This extends beyond the paper's evidence. Failure criterion: no downstream gain with correct structures, or changes caused equally by irrelevant/swapped structures. The extra replay calls and human decision labels are justified only after P0/P1; changing speech gating directly from a higher labeling score is premature.

**Verdict and coverage.** **Adopt the evaluation decomposition; experiment with richer belief records; defer production integration and close leaderboard judgments.** The most consequential next step is a human-adjudicated audit of proposition semantics, kept separate from coarse activity prediction.
