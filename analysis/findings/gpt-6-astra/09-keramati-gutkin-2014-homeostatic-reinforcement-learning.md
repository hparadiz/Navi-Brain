**Assessment.** Experiment with the paper's separation of controlled state, predicted outcome, and state-dependent reward. The consequential implementation finding is that daydream artifact construction or reuse already reduces novelty/stimulation pressure without verified benefit; no learner consuming those deltas was found. The paper does not validate this as homeostatic RL and explicitly leaves novelty rewards outside its demonstrated scope. The priority is to separate stimulation bookkeeping from evidenced need reduction before learning a reward.

**Mechanism and assumptions.** The internal state is a vector of regulated quantities, \(H_t\), with fixed target \(H^*\). The authors define

\[
D(H_t)=\left(\sum_i |h_i^*-h_{i,t}|^n\right)^{1/m},\qquad
r_t=D(H_t)-D(H_{t+1}),\quad H_{t+1}=H_t+K_t.
\]

Here \(K_t\) is the outcome's effect on internal variables. Reward is constructed from its consequences for the organism, rather than attached permanently to an external object (PDF pp. 3–4, Eqs. 1–2, Fig. 1). A fixed food outcome can therefore be valuable when deprived and harmful when it overshoots the target. The RL state includes internal and external conditions; the framework allows model-free value learning or model-based outcome learning. This is more than multiplying an action score by a current pressure.

Choosing \(n>m>1\) gives nonlinear deprivation sensitivity, competition between different drives, and concavity in beneficial outcome magnitude within the stated domain (pp. 7–9, Eqs. 6–9). These properties depend on the selected drive geometry. They are not consequences of every scalar “need” variable. Units and scaling across temperature, glucose, and other coordinates also require a modeling choice; the bare formula cannot determine their relative importance.

The anticipatory mechanism is particularly useful: an action may initially increase deviation yet obtain higher discounted return by preventing a larger forthcoming disturbance. The ethanol example learns a cue-triggered warming response before the cooling event (pp. 5–7, Figs. 2–3). Thus the policy values a trajectory, not just immediate satiation.

The paper subsequently separates estimated from actual outcome effects. Sensory cues provide \(\widehat K_t\), used for immediate reward computation, while actual \(K_t\) changes the internal state (p. 11, Eq. 10). This explains how reinforcement and satiation can dissociate. An added positive, drive-independent reward term produces persistent overconsumption in the hyperpalatability simulations (pp. 15–17, Eq. 11, Fig. 10). For software, the transferable distinction is between evidence predicting useful work and evidence that useful work occurred; the biological analogy does not supply the latter.

**What the theorem actually establishes.** The discrete proof considers trajectories starting at the same \(H_0\) and ending at \(H^*\), hence terminal drive zero. With \(0\leq\gamma<1\), it obtains

\[
\mathrm{SDR}=D(H_0)-(1-\gamma)\mathrm{SDD},\qquad
\mathrm{SDD}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^tD(H_{t+1}).
\]

Maximizing one objective then minimizes the other (pp. 22–23, Eqs. S1–S5). This algebra is sound under those conditions. For an arbitrary finite trajectory, my derivation retains an additional term, \(-\gamma^T D(H_T)\). An infinite-horizon extension needs the discounted terminal contribution to vanish and the relevant expectations/sums to exist. Neither the identity nor an optimal-policy statement guarantees that a particular learner converges, stays within a lethal boundary, or correctly observes its controlled state.

At \(\gamma=1\), the reward differences telescope to endpoint drive reduction. Equal-endpoint trajectories therefore receive equal return regardless of intermediate harm. Discounting resolves that degeneracy for this reward definition. It is not necessary for every homeostatic controller: directly minimizing cumulative drive remains meaningful without discounting. Nor is discounted drive exposure generally a geometric shortest-path objective. The discussion extends its intuition to hyperbolic discounting (p. 5), but the printed proof uses geometric weights; general decreasing weights produce drive penalties proportional to successive weight differences, not automatically the same original discount sequence.

**Evidence and its limits.** This is a mathematical and simulation paper interpreting previously published experiments, not a new animal intervention study.

- Conditioned ethanol tolerance and extinction reproduce the qualitative temperature/response pattern of Mansfield and Cunningham; simulation plots average 500 agents (pp. 6–7, Figs. 2–3). The artificial task supplies the relevant actions and temperature dynamics.
- Equal-mean, different-risk outcomes produce a preference for the safer option under the chosen nonlinear drive function (pp. 8–9, Fig. 5). This establishes model behavior, not a fitted general theory of human risk preferences.
- Oral versus intragastric water simulations distinguish learned reinforcement from satiation and generate rise–fall responding (pp. 11–14, Figs. 7–9). Crucial sensory estimates are assigned by condition, including zero estimated outcome for the fistula route; the result is conditional on that representation.
- Hyperpalatability simulations show the consequences of adding a positive reward bonus; a within-session outcome reduction from 1 g to 0.125 g predicts a transient response burst compared with a simple negative-feedback model (pp. 16–17, 20–21, Figs. 10, 12). The latter is a prediction, not an experimentally confirmed finding in this article.

The comparisons establish explanatory possibility and discriminating predictions. The local article does not provide a common held-out quantitative fit, parameter-identifiability analysis, or comprehensive competition against modern predictive controllers. Hypothalamic/orexin/dopamine discussion supplies biological plausibility, not a measurement of Eq. 2 being implemented by those circuits (p. 15).

Two stronger claims deserve explicit disagreement. First, “stepping back from the brink” is not a safety proof (pp. 9–11, Fig. 6). A one-agent simulation reports no deviation above 40 over \(10^6\) trials, whereas the \(10^5\)-agent, 1,500-trial cohort reaches a maximum of 61. Finite samples and bounded empirical moments do not establish a hard inaccessible boundary. The probability of a particular uninterrupted outward run becoming small does not eliminate its probability or account for all boundary-reaching paths. An irreversible one-step hazard is also outside that favorable small-step example.

Second, unbiased outcome estimates do not generally preserve the nonlinear reward objective, despite the suggestion on p. 11. For the admissible one-dimensional quadratic case, if \(\widehat K=K+\epsilon\), \(E[\epsilon]=0\), and \(K\) is fixed, then

\[
E[\widehat r]=r-E[\epsilon^2].
\]

Action-dependent estimation variance can change action rankings. Thus calibration of mean predicted effects alone is insufficient; one needs the distribution of outcomes or directly calibrated expected drive reduction. This is an analytical observation, not an experiment performed for this review.

There are also visible mathematical presentation errors. Equation 8 prints a positive derivative where the accompanying inhibitory claim requires a negative sign in the stated beneficial-outcome domain. Page 3 calls \(m=n=1\) Euclidean distance, although Eq. 1 gives L1 distance. Page 23 introduces a quadratic derivation with \(m/n=1\), inconsistent with Eq. 1's one-dimensional exponent \(n/m\). These were checked against rendered PDF pages; they should not be copied into implementation. They do not invalidate the discrete telescoping identity.

2. **P1 — Compare drive reduction with simpler control in an offline environment.** After P0, mirror the verified `ActionSelector::choose`/`DecisionStateMachine::integrate` choices in an isolated evaluation using one externally supplied, observable workload and fixed computation budget. Compare the current scoring baseline, a threshold controller, immediate \(D_t-D_{t+1}\), and a bounded-horizon controller using the same observations/actions. Measure integrated backlog/age, task quality, resource use, and worst deviations. Include a cue predicting a workload surge, cue omission, saturation, and delayed action effects. Predefine a meaningful improvement margin; reject adoption if planning fails to beat the simpler controllers at matched budget or degrades urgent-response latency. Discount by elapsed time for variable-duration software actions, not simply by model-call count. Cost: an explicit workload/transition model; this is speculative engineering transfer, not a reproduction of physiology.

3. **P2 — Challenge the predicted-benefit channel before enabling learned selection.** If P1 succeeds, add an action-conditioned effect estimator separate from `ProceduralMemory::simulateCandidate:174` contract feasibility, with predicted and observed deltas linked through existing action traces. Compare fixed mean estimates, calibrated outcome distributions, and observed-benefit updates. Perturb cue usefulness, estimator variance, and add a synthetic fixed “interestingness” bonus while holding actual benefit constant, following Eq. 10 and the hyperpalatability manipulation. Require preference to fall after repeated verified null benefit, bounded calibration error on held-out outcomes, and no increase in useless work or maximum deviation. Preserve independently enforced budgets and installed-action restrictions: scalar reward cannot supply a hard viability guarantee. Cost: outcome coverage and delayed-credit handling, substantially beyond current adapter-contract simulation.

**Verdict: experiment; do not adopt wholesale.** The durable contribution is the causal chain from measured state through predicted outcomes to learned, state-dependent value, together with its failure when reward cues detach from actual benefit. First separate daydream occurrence credit from independently measured novelty or useful outcomes. Then compare a bounded homeostatic objective with simpler operational control; today's pressure counters are not HRL, and finite simulations are not a safety argument.
